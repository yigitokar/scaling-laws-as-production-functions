\section[Economic Implications: Additional Results]{Appendix \thesection. Economic Implications: Additional Results}\label{app:econ}

This appendix supports Section~\ref{sec:econ}. Every estimate is regenerated by one deterministic script in the
replication package from the clean sample and decision units of Section~\ref{sec:wedge}, the technologies and IsoFLOP
minima of Section~\ref{sec:tech}, and two vintages of the Epoch AI database; values taken from the literature are cited
where they appear.

\textit{Compute growth and the vintage of the database.} Table~\ref{tab:app-econ-growth} applies one
estimator, the running top ten of training compute at release with a wild cluster bootstrap by developer, to Epoch AI's
public file as captured on May 31, 2024 \citep{epochai2024dataarchive} and to the snapshot of September 23, 2026
\citep{epochai2026data}. On the 2024 file the estimator gives 4.13-fold growth, consistent with the published 4.2-fold
rate of \citet{sevilla2024training} ($p=0.73$). A two-factor Shapley decomposition attributes 64 percent of the gap to
the 2026 file's 5.23-fold rate to revised compute estimates and 36 percent to entries added after May 2024.

\textit{The trend in the revealed wedge.} Table~\ref{tab:app-econ-trend}, panel~A, gives the trend under alternative
estimators: within developers, above $10^{24}$ FLOP, weighted by compute, for the four disclosed flagship runs above
$10^{25}$ FLOP, and on version 3's 56 family-label units. Over the decision units every interval includes no growth,
except that of the within-developer trend conditional on log compute (2.70-fold a year). Panel~B gives the
compute-weighted wedge by release year, and panel~C the five-year multiples of the scenario of Table~\ref{tab:econ},
panel~B, when the frontier's wedge keeps rising.

\textit{The data wall at observed allocations.} A developer with wedge $w$ minimizes lifetime cost $6ND+\Xi N$, where
$\Xi N$ is its value of compactness, subject to reaching the loss of its unconstrained choice; $\Xi$ is set so that the
unconstrained choice has $\varepsilon_N/\varepsilon_D=w$. Effective data follow \citet{muennighoff2023scaling}, and
the technology is the reference or a member of its observationally equivalent family with the same expansion path and
frontier. All objects depend on $w$ and scarcity only. Table~\ref{tab:app-econ-wall} reports the extra lifetime cost by
$w$ and $r$ (panel~A), the measured wedge under a cap (panel~B), today's frontier under each technology and at the stock's
lower bound (panel~C), and, for compute-optimal runs, the hard wall, the shadow value, the elasticity of substitution
between compute and unique tokens, and the return to compute behind the wall (panel~D). Figure~\ref{fig:app-econ} plots
the main relations. Two sensitivities are worth stating. If the frontier's wedge had followed the open-weight trend since
the median release date of the four anchor runs (mid-2024), frontier runs would already process 3.5 to 8.9 times the
compute-optimal tokens ($\sigma^*=0.60$ to 0.74; 6.2 at 0.70). They would have reached the 100-trillion-token stock
between late 2025 and early 2026, so starting the rise in September 2026, as Section~\ref{sec:econ} does, is the
conservative choice. And in the full repetition model the decay of excess parameters acts as a size penalty, so that
measured wedges of models near the expansion path rise rather than fall as the wall binds (for the frontier with its
wedge held, $\hat w/w$ is 1.04 in 2029 and 1.11 in 2030, against 0.94 and 0.87 under data decay only). Because data are
nonrival \citep{jones2020nonrivalry}, the private shadow values of unique tokens in Section~\ref{sec:econ-wall}
understate their social value when several developers use the same tokens.

\textit{The fleet share of AI compute.} Read as planned serving expenditure, an interpretation that the serving evidence
of Section~\ref{sec:wedge} does not support, the 2025 compute-weighted wedge of the decision units ($w-1=4.4$) implies a
share of AI compute spent on inference. Suppose that each model is served for one year while training compute grows
5.06-fold a year, that a FLOP costs the same in serving as in training, and that final training runs are 10 to 23
percent of developers' research compute \citep{denain2026final}. The implied inference share is then 0.17 to 0.33 (0.68
for one-year lives if all AI compute were final training runs). Longer service lives lower it, because the models in
service are then older vintages trained with less compute: it is 0.11 to 0.23 with two-year lives and 0.08 to 0.17 with
three-year lives (Table~\ref{tab:app-econ-gamma}, panel~C). Without Alibaba the one-year range is 0.09 to 0.19. Disclosures are higher: inference took about
three-fifths of Google's machine-learning energy in 2019--2021 \citep{patterson2022carbon} and 70 percent of Meta's AI
power capacity \citep{wu2022sustainable}, and it was about 40 percent of Nvidia's data-center revenue in fiscal 2024
\citep{nvidia2024q4call}. The comparison is a loose check, and the 2025 wedge rests largely on one family, Alibaba's
Qwen3.

\textit{Growth calibration, fleet shares and synthetic tokens.} Table~\ref{tab:app-econ-gamma} reports $\gamma$ and
the compute needed to halve reducible loss for every large design (panel~A); profiles of the IsoFLOP frontier in the
irreducible loss $E$ (panel~B), which bound $E$ tightly for Chinchilla and Llama~3 but loosely or not at all for Marin's
two-decade ladders, and of which only Chinchilla's is in known loss units and contains its design's joint-fit $E$; the
fleet share of inference implied by the 2025 wedge for service lives of one to three years, R\&D multiples of final
training runs and relative prices of an inference FLOP (panel~C); and the break-even scarcities at which generating a
token costs its shadow value (panel~D).
