\section[The Revealed Value of Compactness: Robustness]{Appendix \thesection. The Revealed Value of Compactness: Robustness}\label{app:wedge}
\setlength{\emergencystretch}{2em}% allow looser lines in the online appendix

This appendix supports Section~\ref{sec:wedge}. Unless stated otherwise, wedges are evaluated under the reference
technology, the Chinchilla form with the outer exponent free, fitted in total parameters like the models' $M$
($\sigma^*_\kappa=0.701$; 0.700 refitted in FLOP-effective parameters, Online Appendix~\ref{app:add-chinchilla}), on the
77 models of the clean sample, which form 49 decision units. Intervals for sample statistics come from the joint
distribution across bootstrap draws of the technology: each draw recomputes the whole sample, including the family
shares.

\subsection{What the Wedge Identifies}\label{app:wedge-cases}

Table~\ref{tab:app-wedge-cases} lists what the wedge $w=\varepsilon_N/\varepsilon_D$ and the share $s=(w-1)/w$ measure
under the objectives of Propositions~\ref{prop:A-wedge} and~\ref{prop:A-family}. With no binding cap on tokens,
$w-1=v_N$, the value of compactness: the objective's own value $m_N$ plus the shadow value of any binding constraint on
size, per unit of training expenditure. When a developer bears the cost of serving its model and no size constraint
binds, $w-1$ is internalized serving expenditure relative to training expenditure, which needs no conversion of FLOPs
into dollars. Binding memory or tier caps and latency objectives are part of the value of compactness and make the wedge
an upper bound on the objective's own $m_N$; data costs, data caps, distillation and binding corpus caps make it a lower
bound. The last row is the factor-bias critique of markup estimation, which the developers' own technologies, the spread
across technologies and a second output on the same runs address (Appendices~\ref{app:wedge-family},
\ref{app:wedge-tech} and~\ref{app:wedge-output}).

\subsection{The Clean Sample}\label{app:wedge-sample}

The clean sample starts from the 173 general-purpose open-weight models of the verified sample (Online
Appendix~\ref{app:choices}). Nine steps, applied in order, drop models whose $(N,D)$ is not the choice that
Proposition~\ref{prop:wedge} describes: (a) repeated observations of one pretraining run (8 models); (b1) research suites
whose $D$ is fixed by design, such as Pythia, OPT and BLOOM (45); (b2) replications and data benchmarks (8); (c)
mixture-of-experts models (12, bounded in Appendix~\ref{app:wedge-conv}); (d) distilled, pruned or derived
initializations, such as Gemma~2 2B and 9B, Gemma~3 and Llama~3.2 (10); (e) multimodal pretraining and (g)
non-transformers, already removed; (f) models without a base checkpoint (7); and (h) token counts without a primary
source (6). The replication package lists every flag with its source. The result is 77 models from 36 families and 18
developers, released 2023--2025. An audit of token counts corrected StableLM-Alpha (800 billion tokens, not the
1.5-trillion-token dataset) and MPT-30B (1.05 trillion: 1 trillion at a context of 2,048 tokens and 50 billion at 8,192);
every table of this appendix uses the audited counts.

The rules implement comments on an earlier draft of this paper, which had reported wedges for all 173 verified models
under 12 of the present 32 technologies (marked in Table~\ref{tab:app-technologies}); neither the rules nor the set of
technologies was therefore fixed before any wedge had been computed. Table~\ref{tab:app-cleaning}, Panel~A, follows the
headline through the steps: the median wedge stays between 3.50 and 4.46. The sign is less robust than the point
estimates suggest: all 32 technologies agree on $w>1$ for 62 percent of clean models (64 percent with the token counts of
the previous version), but the union of their 95 percent intervals lies above one for only 18 percent. Panel~B splits the
sample. Dropping the nine models trained partly on teacher-generated synthetic data (phi-1.5, phi-2 and SmolLM 1--3) gives
a median share of 0.74. The reading protocol's rule is broader: it flags every decision whose report documents
teacher-generated data in pretraining or mid-training, 21 of the 49 decisions with 38 models, including all six decisions
of 2025, and the 39 unflagged models have a median share of 0.62. Models whose size was served by their developer have a
median share of 0.83, against 0.74 for the others; the nine models with an on-device target, from two developers, have
0.90; and models inside memory-tier windows have 0.68, against 0.86 outside. Inside Chinchilla's range of $M$
($M\le341$) the median share is 0.56, and only 2 of the 77 models lie inside Chinchilla's design in both $M$ and compute.

\subsection{Technologies}\label{app:wedge-tech}

\textit{The inclusion rule.} The set of technologies contains (i) every technology estimated in Section~\ref{sec:tech} and
Online Appendix~\ref{app:additional} from final-checkpoint losses of a designed sweep with the primary estimator, each
corpus and parameter-count convention as its own row, under $\kappa=1$ and, where estimated, with $\kappa$ free; (ii) for
each IsoFLOP design, the argmin path with its $\kappa=1$ curvature, the primal fit, and the path combined with the
design's model-free curvature; and (iii) the literature's estimates on the Chinchilla data and the allocation laws
published by Meta, DeepSeek \citep{bi2024deepseek} and MiniCPM \citep{hu2024minicpm}. DataDecide, the unannealed
profiles of \citet{porian2024resolving} and the checkpoint-interpolated profiles that \citet{czech2026problems} build
from the runs of \citet{li2025misfitting}, whose losses come before the learning-rate schedule ends, are excluded, as are
estimator or sample variants of a design already in the set. When a path and a curvature come from different sources,
the inner exponents are $\alpha=S(1-a)$ and $\beta=Sa$ with $S=2(1/\sigma^*-1)$; the wedge depends on the technology
only through $S$, the path slope and the path level (Corollary~\ref{cor:suff}). MiniCPM's compute-optimal ratio of 192 at
$10^{21}$ FLOP lies outside its own design (ratios of 10 to 60), so it is itself an extrapolation.

\textit{What drives the dispersion.} Table~\ref{tab:app-technologies} lists the 32 technologies. The median share ranges
from 0.14 to 0.84 over models and from 0.04 to 0.80 over the 49 decision units. The reference, Farseer with $\kappa$ free,
DeepSeek's law and the developers' paths with model-free curvature give 0.67 to 0.79 over models (0.62 to 0.75 over
decision units); the $\kappa$-free fits of the smaller designs of Gadre et al., the OLMo ladder and Muennighoff et al.\
give 0.74 to 0.84 (0.66 to 0.80). The low medians come from $\kappa=1$ curvatures far above the model-free estimates, as in
Marin's argmin laws ($\sigma^*$ of 0.87 to 0.90), from the OLMo ladder with $\kappa=1$, whose $M^*(10^{24})$ of 291 puts a
quarter of the sample below one, and from MiniCPM's zero point. Estimation choices within the reference design matter as
much as the functional form: imposing $\kappa=1$ lowers the median model wedge from 3.99 to 3.34, and within that form
keeping the five outlying Chinchilla runs raises it to 5.50. Curvature governs levels, not signs: holding the reference
zero point fixed, lowering $\sigma^*$ from 0.80 to 0.60 raises the median wedge from 2.25 to 8.64, while the share of
models with $w>1$, 0.974, does not move. With the curvature of the $\kappa$-free fit to Chinchilla's 137 profile runs
($\sigma^*$ of 0.656 in FLOP-effective parameters) and the reference zero points held fixed, the median decision share is
0.80 rather than 0.73: that fit lowers $\sigma^*$ and raises the levels. Only $\sigma^*$ is available for that fit, so this
is a curvature sensitivity rather than a refitted technology. For the reference, the 95 percent interval for the median
wedge is $[3.21,4.86]$ under the design-conditional wild bootstrap. Pairs resampling, which changes the design, widens its
upper end to 6.04.

\subsection{Parameter Conventions and Mixture-of-Experts Models}\label{app:wedge-conv}

Each technology is evaluated at the model's own parameter count in the technology's convention. A non-embedding technology
ignores the output head, which costs $2N_{\rm head}$ FLOPs per token in training and serving alike, so counting it turns the
lifetime-to-training ratio into $w(1+N_{\rm head}/N_{\rm ne})$. Table~\ref{tab:app-conventions}, Panel~A, shows that the
sample median moves little across conventions, because embeddings are at most 28 percent of parameters in the clean
sample. For the eight clean models below one billion parameters, comparing like with like, the non-embedding count lowers
the median wedge by 8 percent under the Chinchilla form and by 36 percent under the Farseer form. The Farseer change
exceeds, and the Chinchilla change matches, the effect of tokenizer differences on $D$: under the reference
$\partial\ln w/\partial\ln D=0.43$, so a 20 percent difference in tokens per byte moves the wedge by about 8 percent.
Panel~B bounds the wedges of the 12 excluded mixture-of-experts models. A dense technology evaluated at active parameters
treats a mixture of experts as parameter-augmenting and overstates its wedge; at total parameters it ignores the loss
advantage of sparsity \citep{clark2022unified,krajewski2024scaling,abnar2025parameters}. The median is 5.14 at active and
2.07 at total parameters; DeepSeek-V3 has 3.50 or 1.01.

\subsection{Decision Units, Family Readings and the Developers' Own Technologies}\label{app:wedge-family}\label{app:wedge-labown}

\textit{Decision units.} Members of a family whose token budgets are within 10 percent of each other form one decision:
the 77 models form 17 common-budget decisions with 45 models, 17 members of families with size-specific budgets and 15
single-model releases, 49 in all. By Proposition~\ref{prop:A-family}, a budget chosen for its members reveals one number,
the family share $1-1/\bar w_H$ with $\bar w_H$ the compute-weighted harmonic mean of the member wedges, whether the sizes
are chosen at the margin or set by binding size constraints; member wedges differ only through the technology.

\textit{Readings.} Each common budget is read from the developers' reports, release posts and model cards under a coding
protocol written before any decision was coded; the protocol, its three dated amendments (made during coding and before
any class count or median was computed) and every quote, with its URL and retrieval date, are in the replication package.
A \emph{cap} requires evidence that the budget is the developer's whole available corpus, that the corpus was repeated,
or that data limited the budget; a \emph{choice}, a stated cost, compute or schedule reason, a larger usable pool, or other
models trained longer on the same data; a \emph{menu}, sizes set by a hardware or configuration menu. Where the reports
state only the corpus or budget size, or give evidence of both, the reading is \emph{cap or choice}. Statements that a
model was still improving, data expansion and training beyond the compute-optimal point are not evidence on the token
margin. A second coder, who saw the saved sources but not the wedges, coded every decision independently. The readings
agree for 15 of the 17 common budgets (Cohen's kappa 0.83), the flags for a member's own binding cap for 96 percent of
the decisions (kappa 0.90) and the stated sizing rationales with kappas of 0.85 to 1.00. Disagreements were resolved
under the protocol's rules by a reviewer who had seen the wedges; both disagreements on a reading (Granite~3.0 and the
Llama~3 herd) were resolved to the more conservative class, cap or choice.

Table~\ref{tab:app-readings} lists the readings. Choice readings (LLaMA 7B and 13B, Llama~2, StableLM-Alpha, OLMo~2 1B
and 7B, Apertus, Olmo~3) and menu readings (MPT, whose 30B size was chosen to fit one GPU; Yi, sized for consumer GPUs;
Qwen2 1.5B, 7B and 72B) make the family share a point estimate of the value of compactness, since a binding limit on size
is part of that value. A binding cap on tokens makes it a lower bound (Proposition~\ref{prop:A-family}(vi)): so do the
cap-or-choice readings (LLaMA 30B and 65B, Qwen 14B and 72B, the Llama~3 herd, Qwen2.5, Granite~3.0, Qwen3), which are
point estimates only if the budget was chosen, and DeepSeek LLM's cap and menu. Twelve members of size-specific families
and single-model releases with their own binding cap on unique tokens are also lower bounds: OLMo~2 13B and 32B (1.2 and
1.5 epochs of the stage-1 mix), OLMo 1B and 7B, SmolLM 1.7B, SmolLM2 1.7B (about two epochs), phi-1.5, phi-2,
StableLM-3B-4E1T (four epochs), TinyLlama v1.1, and Falcon 40B and 180B. SmolLM 135M and 360M repeated their
252-billion-token corpus about 2.4 times and are lower bounds under either reading. In all, 29 decisions are point
estimates and 20 are lower bounds, 6 of which would be point estimates if their budget was chosen. A member's own $w-1$
bounds its own value of compactness from below only if that member would have trained longer at its own size
(Proposition~\ref{prop:A-family}(vi)), which a binding cap on the family's budget does not imply.

\textit{The Llama~3 herd and OLMo~2.} Family shares run from 0.08 (LLaMA 30B and 65B) to 0.89 (Qwen3). Because the
flagship carries most of a family's compute, they lie far below the members' own shares: the Llama~3 herd's member wedges
run from 1.29 to 6.73, but its weighted condition gives $\bar w_H=1.42$, a share of 0.29, and 0.38 under Meta's own
eight-budget path. The herd's 95 percent interval is $[0.08,0.46]$. The report and the release post
\citep{grattafiori2024llama,meta2024llama3} support a cap reading, and the report attributes the smaller models' long
training to inference efficiency; the protocol codes the herd as cap or choice because neither source states that the
corpus bound. Under a cap, 0.29 bounds the herd's value of compactness from below, and the 8B model's share is at least
0.85 under the reference (0.89 on Meta's path) if the 8B would have trained longer at its own size ($v_N\ge w-1=5.73$).
The flagship's sign is not identified beyond the designs (Appendix~\ref{app:wedge-pi}). OLMo~2's 1B and 7B models share a
budget of 4.05 trillion tokens, and its 13B and 32B repeated part of the stage-1 mix. Its member shares fall from 0.87 to
0.61 under the reference and from 0.80 to 0.12 under AI2's own ladder path with the common curvature; the ladder's
allocation exponent is imprecise (a bootstrap interval of [0.11, 0.82] with $\kappa=1$). OLMo~2's tokens are counted as
pretraining plus the mean mid-training budget of the averaged runs (Online Appendix~\ref{app:choices}).

\textit{Unit definitions and classes.} Over the 49 decisions the median share is 0.735, with a joint wild-bootstrap
interval of $[0.657,0.782]$. Under a bootstrap that resamples the Chinchilla runs by budget and model-size trunk (44
clusters) the interval widens to $[0.512,0.806]$, so the statement that the value of compactness exceeded training cost
at the margin for the median decision ($s>0.5$) survives only barely. The 29 point-identified decisions have a median of
0.737, with an interval of $[0.672,0.790]$, and the 20 lower bounds 0.710. Under the cluster bootstrap the
point-identified interval is $[0.539,0.824]$. The point-identified median is 0.735 over 35 decisions if every
cap-or-choice budget without repetition was chosen, 0.737 under the second coder's readings (31 decisions) and 0.745
without MPT and Yi, whose agreed codes rest on the weakest evidence (27). Without synthetic-flagged decisions it is
0.675 under the protocol's rule (18) and 0.678 without SmolLM 1--3, phi-1.5, phi-2, Qwen2.5, Qwen3 and OLMo~2 (25). Over
the 17 decisions outside tier windows, an interpretation check rather than a condition for identification, the median is
0.837. Other unit definitions give 0.736 over the 56 units of the earlier version, which grouped only families trained
entirely on one budget; 0.751 when the eight cap-type budgets are split into their members (66 units; 0.752 for the
earlier version's 66 reading-consistent units); 0.736 with OLMo~2 1B as a decision of its own; and 0.749 over the 77
models. The median is 0.737 without Alibaba's models (43 decisions), 0.735 without Qwen2.5 and Qwen3, whose per-member
token counts are not audited, and 0.735 with StableLM-3B-4E1T and TinyLlama at the effective data of
\citet{muennighoff2023scaling} (their wedges move from 5.96 to 5.78 and from 6.57 to 6.52). The token audit moves the
StableLM-Alpha member wedges from 3.79 to 2.66 and from 2.65 to 1.92, and MPT-30B's from 1.20 to 1.22 (the MPT family
share from 0.24 to 0.25), but not the median, which is 0.735 with the uncorrected counts.

\textit{The developers' own technologies under one rule.} Where a developer has published scaling evidence, one rule
builds its technology: the developer's own expansion path, in its own parameter convention (the OLMo ladder without the
input embedding, DeepSeek's law in non-embedding FLOPs per token), combined with the common model-free $\sigma^*=0.687$.
For Meta the path is fitted to the eight IsoFLOP budgets that bracket their minimum; for Marin it is the Nemotron-CC
ladder, for AI2 the OLMo ladder with $\kappa$ free, and for DeepSeek its published law. Llama~1 and~2 are left out of the
lab-own set, whose 2024 path their developers could not have used; 15 models remain. Table~\ref{tab:app-labown} shows that
the median share is 0.53 under the one rule against 0.70 under the reference, and 0.71 against 0.65 without AI2's models,
so the order reverses. AI2 drives the gap: its ladder path puts $M^*$ at 82 to 145 at these models' compute, 3.7 to 6.7
times the reference, and its models' median share is 0.44 with the common curvature against 0.66 with the ladder's own.
On Meta's bracketed path, Llama~3 8B has $w=8.75$, with a 95 percent interval of $[4.58,18.13]$, and Llama~3.1 405B has
1.47; the 405B is on Meta's path ($w=0.97$) only under the ten-budget planning law, which uses two unbracketed budgets.
Over the 49 decisions, with developers' own technologies where they exist and the reference elsewhere, the median is
0.717. As for the reference, the lab-own levels are lower bounds if $\sigma^*$ falls with compute.

\subsection{Serving, Conduct and Tiers}\label{app:wedge-conduct}

\textit{Serving coded at the model level.} Online Appendix~\ref{app:choices} gives the coding rule. Twenty-six of the 77
models were served by their developer at release, 19 of them Alibaba's (every Qwen size was in Alibaba's own catalog, the
smallest Qwen2.5 sizes at a zero price). Seven models that the previous, developer-level code counted as served are not:
Gemma 2B and 7B (self-deploy catalog only), DeepSeek LLM 7B (the chat product served only the 67B), phi-1.5, phi-2, and
Llama~3 8B and 70B, whose consumer product did not name a size. Twenty-five of the 26 served models share a budget with
other sizes or sit in a tier window, which makes their wedges family-level or partly a tier's shadow value; only Qwen2
0.5B is neither (Qwen2 1.5B shares its 7T budget with the 7B and 72B).

\textit{Tests.} Table~\ref{tab:app-conduct} reports restricted wild cluster bootstrap tests by developer
\citep{cameron2008bootstrap,webb2023reworking}. Coded by model, serving has no detectable relation to the wedge at given
compute and year: the coefficient is 0.56 (s.e.\ 0.24), with a $p$-value of 0.41 from six treated developers, and it is
$-0.09$ without Alibaba. At the decision level it is 0.28 ($p=0.67$). Randomization inference that permutes the
model-level code within year-by-compute strata rejects ($p<0.001$ for the coefficient, 0.06 for its $t$-statistic), but it
treats each size as a separate assignment, although one developer decides for all its sizes and 19 of the 26 served
models are Alibaba's. Permuting the developers' shares of served models across developers
\citep{mackinnon2020randomization} gives 0.10, and 0.94 without Alibaba. Open weights carry a premium: at the decision
level, with the clean sample's common budgets entered once, open-weight decisions have log wedges 0.45 higher
($p=0.018$), and 0.63 higher among releases since 2023 ($p=0.001$). Over-training is highest, by deployment target, for
models built for devices their developers do not serve (median share 0.90 against 0.70), although the single most
over-trained decision, Qwen2 0.5B, was served; the contrast is descriptive and rests on two developers.

\textit{Tiers and derivatives.} Windows around quantized memory boundaries hold 51 percent of the 220 open-weight
production-scale models released in 2023--2026, against 13 percent under a smooth counterfactual
\citep{chetty2011adjustment,kleven2016bunching}, but position relative to the tiers does not predict the wedge beyond the
mechanical relation between $N$ and $\ln w$ when compared with randomly shifted tier menus. Models with higher $M$ at given
compute have more derivative repositories on Hugging Face (elasticity 0.80, $p$-value 0.031), as planned demand predicts
but also as accessibility predicts; the elasticity with respect to planned $T/D$ is only 0.60 ($p$-value 0.086), and the
verified sample selects on popularity. These two tests use the token counts and serving code of the previous version.

\textit{The reading the data favor.} A developer that bears all of its serving cost would over-train more the more it
serves, and a developer that serves no one would over-train less. The data show no detectable serving effect once Alibaba
is set aside, over-training is highest, by deployment target, for the on-device models that their developers do not
serve, and open weights carry a premium of about half a log point. They are more consistent with the adoption and tier
mechanisms (Proposition~\ref{prop:A-wedge}(iii)(a$'$) and (b)) than with serving-cost internalization.

\subsection{Extrapolation and Partial Identification}\label{app:wedge-pi}

\textit{Extrapolation in $M$.} Table~\ref{tab:app-extrap} compares the local wedge of
Proposition~\ref{prop:A-modelfree}(i), from a kernel-weighted local quadratic of the loss surface, with the wedges of
parametric forms fitted to the same runs, in three recipes. In each, parametric forms fitted to the whole design understate
the local wedge at $M$ of 256 to 1,024, and the gap grows with $M$. The $\kappa$-free form's log gap there is $-0.14$ on
Farseer, $-0.43$ on Marin's three corpora pooled (s.e.\ 0.03) and $-0.38$ on Llama~3 (two evaluation points); a
Chinchilla form fitted to runs with $M\le100$ gives $-0.95$, $-1.03$ and $-0.90$. Measured against a linear extrapolation
from the local slope at the path, the form of the $\kappa$ family, the local $\ln w$ is convex on Farseer and Marin, by
0.17 and 0.43 log points at $M$ of 256 to 1,024. On Marin that is the whole of the $\kappa$-free form's gap; leaving out the
corner runs of each budget gives 0.38 to 0.46, but the bin then stops at $M$ of about 320 or 595, so the change reflects
which points remain. On Llama~3 the local $\ln w$ is concave and bandwidth-sensitive ($-0.13$ at $M$ of 64 to 256, about
zero at its two points near $M=290$), and its gap comes from a steeper local slope at the path. The local wedge is
evaluated only up to $M\approx2{,}570$ on Farseer, 1,110 on Marin and 290 on Llama~3, so the direction of the
extrapolation error is established in three recipes at $10^{18}$ to $10^{22}$ FLOP, not in general. Twenty clean models
lie beyond Farseer's range of $M$ in its non-embedding convention, and the measured inefficiency gradients of Farseer's
tuning rule change its convexity by only $-4$ to $+8$ percent, but they are measured at $M$ up to 466 only, while Farseer's
grid reaches 2,570 (Online Appendix~\ref{app:add-measurement}). Revealed preference recovers the technology a developer
believed, which may be the parametric one (Remark~\ref{rem:A-pi-param}). \textcolor{red}{[TBD-m9: the controlled
experiment's local-wedge check under Amendments~1 and~2.]}

\textit{Extrapolation in compute: the identified set.} No design observes $M^*(C)$ above $10^{22}$ FLOP. For each study
we fit a line to the logged IsoFLOP minima at its bracketed budgets, $\ln M^*_b=a_s+e_s(c_b-c_J)$, and extrapolate it
with a simultaneous 95 percent band that carries the joint sampling error of level and slope: a wild bootstrap-$t$ with
Webb weights and 9,999 draws, studentized by the within-study pooled residual variance, with a sup-$t$ critical value at
least as large as the normal-theory one (notes to Table~\ref{tab:app-sign}). Chinchilla has nine bracketed budgets up to
$3\times10^{21}$ FLOP, in total parameters as digitized, the convention of the models' $M$; Llama~3 has eight up to
$10^{21}$, in FLOP-implied parameters; and Marin's three corpora, which share code and ladder, have seven, seven and
five up to $3\times10^{20}$ FLOP, with corpus-specific levels and one slope, 0.198 (95 percent interval
$[0.132,0.265]$). DeepSeek's published law is a fourth, point path. By Proposition~\ref{prop:A-pi}(vii) and (viii), the
union of the bands, widened by $\ln\tau$, is an identified set for the developer's zero point. It gives
$M^*(10^{24})\in[5.7,248]$. At $10^{25}$ FLOP the set is $[4.5,468]$. With every path and model in total parameters, the
set at $10^{24}$ FLOP is $[4.4,391]$. Marin's band sets the upper end of the set for 76 of the 77 models and Llama~3's
band the lower end for all of them.

\textit{The sign.} By Proposition~\ref{prop:A-pi}(iii), $w>1$ is identified whenever $\ln M$ exceeds the upper end of the
set, for any technology with single-peaked IsoFLOP profiles. Table~\ref{tab:app-sign} reports the share identified as a
function of a tilt allowance $\tau$ on $M^*$ that covers tokenizer differences and data-quality tilts. With no allowance
the sign is identified for 78 percent of models, 69 percent of the 49 decisions and 71 percent of the 56 units of the
earlier version, but for only 24 percent of training compute (14 percent over decisions). No model is identified as below
one, which is mechanical: every clean model has $M\ge19.5$, while the lower end of the set at the models' compute runs
from 4.0 to 11.4. The union also matters for that statement: Marin's band alone would identify $w<1$ for six flagships
(Llama~2 70B, LLaMA 65B, Llama~3.1 405B, Falcon 180B, Falcon 40B and DeepSeek LLM 67B). The 17 models whose sign is not
identified all have $M$ below 250 (the largest is Qwen2.5 72B, at 247.6), and only one of them, LLaMA 13B, has fewer than
15 billion parameters. By year, the compute-weighted identified share is 12 percent in 2023, 19 percent in 2024 and 49
percent in 2025.

\textit{Straight paths.} The paths are straight lines by assumption, and inside the designs they are not: a quadratic
term in $\ln C$ has a $t$-statistic of 7.15 for Chinchilla (convex), 2.39 for Llama~3 (convex) and $-3.11$ for Marin
(concave). At the anchors the local slopes are 0.40 for Chinchilla and 0.25 for Llama~3, against $-0.05$ for Marin, so
Marin's steep line comes from its small budgets and the flat lines of Chinchilla and Llama~3 average a fall and a rise. The
bands cover sampling error around a line, not this misspecification. Continuing Chinchilla's and Llama~3's paths above
their anchors at their local slopes, beside the bands, identifies 75 percent of models and 20 percent of compute, with an
upper end of 339 for $M^*(10^{24})$; with one standard error added to each local slope, 71 and 17 percent, and 477.

\textit{Other identified sets.} Table~\ref{tab:app-sign} also reports sets under weaker or different restrictions.
An earlier version of this paper (PI-1) put each design's level on a bootstrap-$t$ interval at its largest bracketed
budget and bounded the path slopes by the point estimates of the paths and published laws, $[-0.156,0.162]$; it
identified 87 percent of models and 48 percent of compute. It excluded Marin's Comma corpus, whose five bracketed budgets
give the interval $[0.2,3{,}586]$ for its anchor, by a rule of at least six budgets that was chosen after that interval
was seen; with Comma only 8 percent of models were identified. Levels on $t$-intervals with $k-2$ degrees of freedom,
with Comma admitted with its own slope of 0.294, identify 68 percent of models and 17 percent of compute; PI-1's levels
with slopes anywhere in the hull of the slopes' 95 percent intervals, $[-0.175,0.353]$, identify 71 and 17 percent. Every
set that carries slope uncertainty or admits Comma with a valid interval lies between 68 and 78 percent of models and 17
and 24 percent of compute, except one that lets every slope reach its own $t$-interval, Comma's 0.459 included: 44 and 9
percent. With normal inputs as the only restriction ($|e_p|\le1$), 13 percent of models are identified (14 with PI-1's
levels); admitting every in-set technology's in-support $M^*$ as an anchor, 23 percent. With anchors and models all in
total parameters the share of models is 71 percent, and all in FLOP-effective parameters 75 percent.

\textit{Tilt allowances and the breakdown frontier.} Figure~\ref{fig:app-breakdown} traces the share identified against
$\tau$. The share of models falls below one half at $\tau=3.7$ (2.7 with PI-1's levels and slopes at their 95 percent
intervals), the share of models below 15 billion parameters at 7.5, and the share of the 49 decisions at 2.7; the compute
share is below one half at every $\tau$. At $\tau=1.84$, DataDecide's largest tilt under the reference exponents, the
shares are 66 percent of models, 61 percent of decisions and 17 percent of compute; at 3.4, the same tilt under
DataDecide's own exponents, 53, 47 and 16 percent.

\textit{Magnitudes and the curvature at frontier compute.} Magnitudes need a curvature (Proposition~\ref{prop:A-pi}(iv)).
With $1/\sigma^*-1$ between 0.40 and 0.68, from the in-design estimates (0.40 to 0.52) to the top-budget value
($\sigma^*=0.594$), the medians of the models' lower and upper bounds on $s$ are 0.41 and 0.95 (0.41 and 0.90 with 0.40
to 0.52); tilt allowances of 1.84 and 3.4 lower the median lower bound to 0.24 and 0.03. Over the 49 decisions, whose
bounds are the compute-weighted harmonic means of their members' bounds, the medians are 0.33 and 0.88 with 0.40 to
0.52, and the median lower bound is 0.15 at $\tau=1.84$ and $-0.12$ at 3.4: at DataDecide's own factor the median
decision's sign is not identified. The curvature itself falls with compute in the two IsoFLOP designs that reach
$10^{21}$ FLOP (Online Appendix~\ref{app:add-modelfree}). Beyond the designs, $\sigma^*(C)$ is partially identified
\citep{manski2003partial}: it lies between the value at the largest budgets and a linear continuation of the
Chinchilla--Llama~3 drift if it does not rise with compute and falls no faster than where it is observed
(Section~\ref{sec:tech}; Table~\ref{tab:app-sigmaC-wedge}). With $\sigma^*$ at the lower end of that set at each model's
compute, the median upper bound on $s$ is 0.99. We also propagate constant values of $\sigma^*$ into the wedges with each
decision's reference zero point held fixed. Because $\ln w$ rises with $1/\sigma^*-1$ when $w>1$, the reference levels
are lower bounds if $\sigma^*$ falls with compute: the median decision share is 0.73 at the reference, 0.87 at
$\sigma^*=0.60$, 0.85 at 0.62 and 0.96 with $\sigma^*$ at the lower end of its set. The share with $w>1$ does not depend
on the curvature. A drifting $\sigma^*$ also violates the quasi-homotheticity behind the closed forms, so these are scale
propagations, not re-estimations (Table~\ref{tab:app-sigmaC-wedge}).

\subsection{A Second Output}\label{app:wedge-output}

A developer that optimizes a task metric rather than validation loss is misread by an econometrician who uses loss, by the
factor $w_L/w_{L'}$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:A-wedge}(ii). The OLMo ladder's 30 runs are scored both on C4 cross-entropy
and on task bits per byte, so technologies fitted to each output on the same runs measure that factor \citep[a test of the
output critique of][]{bond2020unpleasant}. Table~\ref{tab:app-second-output} reports the median over the 77 clean models of
$\ln(w_{\rm task}/w_{\rm C4})$: $-0.11$ with $\kappa=1$ and $-0.37$ with $\kappa$ free for all tasks. Every 95 percent
interval of the cross-model median includes zero except BoolQ's under $\kappa=1$, whose fit is degenerate; the effect
varies by task family, and neither its sign nor its size is stable across forms. The intervals are wide: with $\kappa$
free, the all-task interval allows a task-valuing developer's wedge from a third of the loss-based one to three and a half
times it. This is one recipe at small scale (190M to 3.2B parameters, $M\le200$): the design has no power, and the output
critique remains unbounded.

\subsection{The Cost Side and Post-Training Compute}\label{app:wedge-cost}

FLOPs are not costs. Table~\ref{tab:app-costsens} lets the price of a training FLOP vary with model size (elasticity
$\delta$) and serving cost per token scale as $N^\phi$, with a price $\varrho$ of a serving FLOP relative to a training
FLOP (Proposition~\ref{prop:A-wedge}(iii)(f)). The share $s=(w-1-\delta)/(w-1-\delta+\phi)$ does not depend on $\varrho$
and moves little: the median is 0.72 to 0.77 for $\delta$ between $-0.1$ and 0.1 with $\phi=1+\delta$, and 0.70 to 0.86
for $\phi$ between 0.5 and 1.25 with $\delta=0$. Token counts depend on $\varrho$: under the serving reading, planned
serving tokens for the median model are 9.0 times training tokens at $\lambda\varrho=1$ and 0.9 times at
$\lambda\varrho=10$, and for the median decision, $T/D=3(w-1)/(\lambda\varrho)$, 8.3 and 0.8.

Post-training compute scales with $N$ at given post-training tokens and does not scale with $D$, so it enters the
first-order condition like $\delta>0$: $w=1+x_P+m_N$, with $x_P$ post-training compute relative to pre-training compute.
Disclosed budgets put $x_P$ at 0.0019 for DeepSeek-V3 \citep{deepseekai2024deepseekv3} and at 0.0004 and 0.0007 for the
final supervised stage of T\"ulu~3 on Llama~3.1 8B and 70B \citep{lambert2024tulu3}, converting disclosed GPU-hours at an
assumed 40 percent utilization. For Olmo~3 Think 32B \citep{olmo2025olmo3}, reinforcement learning took 0.026 of
pre-training compute at the initial release and 0.135 with the 21-day continuation of Olmo~3.1; all post-training is at
most 0.21 of pre-training before the initial release and at most 0.32 including the continuation. At $x_P=0.32$ for every
model the median share falls from 0.749 to 0.727 over models and from 0.735 to 0.710 over decision units, and the 2025
compute-weighted share from 0.83 to 0.82 (to 0.77 if post-training compute is also counted in the denominator,
$m_N/(1+m_N+x_P)$). Post-training compute is second order for the median and trims the 2025 aggregate by about 0.01, or
0.06 under the wider definition.

\subsection{Trends and Validation}\label{app:wedge-trends}

\textit{The trend from 2023.} Table~\ref{tab:app-trend} applies one set of rules to the clean sample in 2023, 2024 and
2025, and Table~\ref{tab:app-trend-boot} adds sampling uncertainty and composition. Under the reference, the
compute-weighted share over decisions is 0.24, 0.54 and 0.81, and over models 0.27, 0.58 and 0.83. It rises in both steps
under every one of the 32 technologies, over models and over decisions, and in all 18 samples that leave one developer
out. A bootstrap that resamples the 18 developers (9,999 draws) gives a 95 percent interval of $[0.19,0.72]$ for the change
over decisions from 2023 to 2025. For the change from 2023 to 2024 the interval is $[0.02,0.65]$. For the step from 2024
to 2025 it is $[-0.12,0.44]$, so that step is not established. The medians behave differently: the median decision share
is 0.49, 0.77 and 0.74 and rises in both steps under only 1 of the 32 technologies, while the median model share, 0.55,
0.82 and 0.87, rises in both under 31. Without any one of Alibaba, Hugging Face or Marin the median decision share falls
from 2024 to 2025 by more, to 0.65 in each case. The trend largely restates the rise in $M$, whose median rose from 146 in
2023 to 2,438 in 2025, since at given compute every technology ranks models by $M$. Llama~3.1 405B, whose sign is not
identified, carries 55 percent of 2024 compute; the compute-weighted identified share is 12, 19 and 49 percent in the
three years, so part of the weighted rise is a shift in composition; and the 2025 cohort has six decision units, all
dense.

\textit{Composition, flags and rationales.} Tier windows hold 47, 44 and 92 percent of clean-sample compute in 2023, 2024
and 2025. Adding the excluded mixture-of-experts models (six in 2024 and six in 2025) as decisions of their own moves the
2025 compute-weighted share over decisions from 0.81 to 0.82 at active parameters and to 0.73 at total parameters (2024:
0.56 and 0.52, against 0.54); once added, they carry 53 percent of 2025 compute. Within developers, a regression of the
log decision wedge on the release date with developer fixed effects gives growth of 1.59-fold a year, with a 95 percent
interval of $[0.91,2.61]$ from the bootstrap over developers (2.70 given log compute), against 1.86 pooled; the slope is
identified by the nine developers with decisions at more than one release date, six of them in more than one year. The
wild cluster bootstrap-$t$ that Section~\ref{sec:econ} uses gives wider intervals for the same regressions,
$[0.47,5.59]$ with developer fixed effects and $[0.96,3.54]$ pooled (Online Appendix Table~\ref{tab:app-econ-trend}). The protocol's synthetic-data rule flags every 2025 decision, so no 2025 share without flagged decisions exists
under it; dropping SmolLM 1--3, phi-1.5, phi-2, Qwen2.5, Qwen3 and OLMo~2 instead leaves three 2025 decisions, with a
compute-weighted share of 0.67. Table~\ref{tab:app-rationale} reports the stated sizing rationales, coded under the
protocol by both coders. The share of decisions citing a compute-optimal target falls from 0.12 in 2023 to 0.04 in 2024
and zero in 2025 (weighted by compute, 0.45, 0.65 and zero, as the Llama~3 herd's flagship dominates 2024); the three
decisions that cite one (the Llama~3 herd, Falcon 40B and 180B) have a median share of 0.04. Among decisions with a
deployment rationale (inference cost, or a device or memory target) the median share rises from 0.48 (six decisions) to
0.83 (18) and 0.89 (three); among decisions that state no rationale it is 0.55, 0.75 and 0.65.

\textit{Before 2023.} The eight open-weight production-scale models of 2019--2022 have a compute-weighted share of 0.04
under the reference technology, but two Kaplan-era flagships carry 92 percent of the period's compute. Under the belief
that developers planned with the allocation law of \citet{kaplan2020scaling} (Proposition~\ref{prop:A-wedge}(ii)), the
share is 0.19 to 0.29 (0.43 to 0.57 with Kaplan's curvature as well), and OPT-175B, a replication of GPT-3, lies on the
believed path by construction. The low pre-2023 value is therefore a belief regime, not a measured absence of value, and
the paper states the trend from 2023.

\textit{Token volumes.} Per-model token volumes on OpenRouter cannot be collected under its terms, and the
developer-level totals of \citet{aubakirova2026state} show that one year of that traffic is a small part of the lifetime
tokens that the releases imply under the serving reading at $\lambda\varrho=1$ (4.6 percent for DeepSeek, 0.04 percent for
Alibaba), which says only that the platform carries a small part of a model's use; a test of levels is not possible with
public data.

\textit{What is not done.} A test on a second margin in the spirit of \citet{raval2023testing}, such as the sparsity of
mixture-of-experts models, the number of key-value heads or the vocabulary, has not been run. Token counts are not
normalized by bytes, and Qwen2.5's per-member token counts are not audited. Durability, the obsolescence of models that
shortens the horizon over which serving demand is planned, is part of $T$ but is not modeled. Loss is not capability: the
second-output test covers one recipe, and the effect of quantization \citep{kumar2024scaling}, which changes deployed
quality without changing training, is signed through the output concept of Proposition~\ref{prop:wedge}(iii) but not
measured.
