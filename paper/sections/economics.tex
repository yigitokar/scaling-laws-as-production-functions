\section{Economic Implications}\label{sec:econ}

Three economic questions turn on the objects estimated in Sections~\ref{sec:tech} and~\ref{sec:wedge}: how fast the
demand for training data grows, what a binding limit on unique text costs, and how much of AI compute goes to serving
models rather than training them. Each loads on a different object. The allocation exponent $a$, the least portable
object, governs the first. The curvature $\sigma^*$, the best-measured object, barely matters for the second. The
level of the third rests on the zero point $M^*(C)$. Table~\ref{tab:econ} and Figure~\ref{fig:econ} collect the
numbers.

\subsection{The Growth of Data Demand}\label{sec:econ-demand}

Along the expansion path, compute-optimal data grow as $D^*(C)\propto C^{1-a}$, where $a$ is the compute elasticity of
the optimal model size (Proposition~\ref{prop:duality}). Frontier training compute has grown 5.1-fold a year since
2018 (95 percent confidence interval 4.3 to 5.9), close to the four- to fivefold rate reported by
\citet{sevilla2024training}.\footnote{We regress $\log_{10}C$ on the release date of the running top-ten training runs
in \citet{epochai2026data} (snapshot of September 23, 2026; 92 runs, 2018--2026); the interval is a wild cluster
bootstrap over 27 developers \citep{webb2023reworking}. Excluding runs whose compute Epoch rates as speculative gives
4.8-fold. For January 2018 to May 2024 we obtain 5.2-fold, against Epoch's published 4.2-fold, probably a vintage
effect of the database.} Across ten technologies, $a$ runs from 0.36 (Farseer's own form, locally at $10^{22}$ to
$10^{23}$ FLOP) to 0.57 (the RefinedWeb sweep of \citealt{gadre2024language}). Compute-optimal data demand therefore
grows 2.0-fold to 2.8-fold a year, or 34-fold to 183-fold over five years; $\sigma^*$ does not enter. The level is not
portable either: at the fitted frontier compute of September 2026, $9.4\times10^{26}$ FLOP, compute-optimal data range
from 9 to 519 trillion tokens. The designs of Section~\ref{sec:tech} agree on curvature but not on the expansion
path, and that disagreement becomes a factor of more than five in five-year data needs.

These forecasts bear on whether frontier training will exhaust public text, whose quality-adjusted stock
\citet{villalobos2022run} put at about 100 trillion tokens in 2024 (95 percent interval 22 to 490 trillion). Because
the technologies disagree about levels, we anchor each at the data use of the four disclosed dense training runs above
$10^{25}$ FLOP. Frontier runs then reach 100 trillion unique tokens between mid-2026 and late 2027 under every
technology, and the repetition-adjusted stock of 320 trillion tokens between late 2027 and mid-2029, close to Villalobos
et al.'s median of 2028 (Figure~\ref{fig:econ}, panel a). The size of the stock dominates the uncertainty: under the
Chinchilla technology, the 5--95 percent range of the first date runs from 2025.7 to 2029.4, whereas $a$ moves the dates
by only about a year.

\subsection{The Data Wall}\label{sec:econ-wall}

A data wall concerns unique tokens, whereas $D$ counts tokens processed, including repetitions. We therefore combine
the technology with the repetition model of \citet{muennighoff2023scaling}, in which repeated tokens lose value
exponentially: effective data are $D'=U+UR^*_D(1-e^{-R/R^*_D})$, where $U$ is unique tokens, $R=D/U-1$ the number of
repetitions, and $R^*_D=15.4$ their estimated decay scale. Scarcity is $r\equiv D^*(C)/U$, the ratio of
compute-optimal to unique tokens; the cost of a cap on $U$ and the shadow value of a unique token,
$-\partial C/\partial U$ at fixed loss, depend on $r$ alone (Table~\ref{tab:econ}, panel B).

The curvature barely matters. At $r=4$, reaching the unconstrained frontier loss costs 7.2 percent more compute under
the Chinchilla refit with $\kappa=1$ ($\sigma^*=0.74$), 7.4 percent with $\kappa$ free ($\sigma^*=0.70$), and 7.7
percent under a member of the $\kappa=1$ refit's observationally equivalent family that shares its expansion path and
frontier but has $\sigma^*=0.60$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:ident}). Curvature matters only near the hard wall, the
scarcity beyond which the frontier loss is unattainable: $r=117$, 83, and 47 for the three technologies. The repetition
technology matters far more. Our primary specification, which uses Muennighoff et al.'s data-decay scale without their
decay of excess parameters, is the most favorable case. Under their full model, in which excess parameters also lose
value, the cost at $r=4$ is 43 percent and the hard wall falls to $r\approx9$; the gap between $\sigma^*=0.74$ and 0.70
stays small (43 versus 40 percent).

The elasticity that governs the wall is thus not $\sigma^*$, which describes substitution between parameters and tokens
processed, but the elasticity of substitution between compute and unique tokens, which combines $\sigma^*$ with the
returns to repetition. Under Muennighoff et al.'s estimates it rises from 0.28 to 0.58 as $r$ increases from 1.5 to
16, almost independently of $\sigma^*$; it is implied by the two models, not estimated. Behind the wall the return to
compute falls, but slowly: the compute elasticity of reducible loss is $0.95\gamma$ at $r=4$ and $0.82\gamma$ at
$r=16$, where $\gamma$ is the frontier elasticity. Whether the wall binds today depends on $M^*$ rather than
$\sigma^*$: at the 2026 frontier, compute-optimal $r$ against a 100-trillion-token stock ranges from 0.09 to 5.2 across
technologies, while frontier runs at observed data use process 0.6 to 1.2 times the stock.

At $10^{26}$ FLOP and $r=4$, a unique token is worth 0.43 of the compute cost of processing one token: about \$3 per
million tokens at \$$10^{-18}$ per FLOP, the median amortized hardware and energy cost of 2024--2025 frontier runs
\citep{epochai2026data}. At $r=16$ it is worth \$88 per million; at $r\le1$, nothing (Figure~\ref{fig:econ}, panel c).
These are private values to one run; because data are nonrival \citep{jones2020nonrivalry}, a token that several
developers use is worth more to society.

Where $\sigma^*$ does matter is in translating serving demand into data demand. At given compute, a model with wedge
$w$ uses $D/D^*(C)=w^{\sigma^*/[2(1-\sigma^*)]}$ times the compute-optimal data, because
$\ln w=(1/\sigma^*-1)\ln[M/M^*(C)]$ in the $\kappa$ family (Proposition~\ref{prop:modelfree}). At $w=3$ the multiple is 4.7 under $\sigma^*=0.74$ but 2.3 under $\sigma^*=0.60$.
Forecasts that start from observed tokens per parameter do not depend on $\sigma^*$; forecasts that start from
projected serving demand do.

\subsection{The Serving Share of AI Compute}\label{sec:econ-share}

Aggregated over models, the share $s=(w-1)/w$ of Section~\ref{sec:wedge} measures the value developers placed on
compact models relative to lifetime cost; for developers that serve their models, it is the planned serving share of
lifetime cost (Proposition~\ref{prop:wedge}). Weighted by training compute, it rose from 0.04 for open-weight models
released in 2019--2022 to 0.54 in 2024 and 0.82 in 2025 (Table~\ref{tab:econ}, panel C). Fleet disclosures are of the
same order: inference took about three-fifths of Google's machine-learning energy in 2019--2021
\citep{patterson2022carbon}, 70 percent of Meta's AI power capacity \citep{wu2022sustainable}, and about 40 percent of
Nvidia's data-center revenue in fiscal 2024 \citep{nvidia2024q4call}.

The objects differ, however, and the differences cut against agreement. The share $s$ compares planned serving with a
model's own final training run, whereas fleet shares mix vintages and include research compute. For OpenAI in 2024,
press-reported inference spending was about a quarter of all compute spending but four-fifths of inference plus final
training runs \citep{you2025openai}. Two adjustments convert the 2025 planned
multiple, $w-1=4.5$, into a fleet share. Fivefold annual growth of training compute roughly halves the serving flow
relative to current training when models are served for a year, and final training runs account for only 10 to 23
percent of developers' research compute \citep{denain2026final}. Together they imply fleet shares of 0.18 to 0.34 at
equal cost per FLOP; the disclosed range is reached only if research compute is small. The comparison is therefore a
loose check, not a validation. Because the level of $s$ depends on the technology but its rising trend does not
(Section~\ref{sec:wedge-trends}), $s$ is a revealed-preference indicator of expected deployment---for energy and
capital-expenditure planning and for the market for inference \citep{demirer2025emerging}---in its trend if not in its
level.

\subsection{Growth Models}\label{sec:econ-growth}

For models of AI and growth that map compute into capability, the relevant elasticity is $\gamma$, not $\sigma^*$:
near the expansion path, compute is a sufficient statistic for the input bundle (Proposition~\ref{prop:duality}), and
$\sigma^*$ enters only when the mix is constrained, as behind a data wall. Halving reducible loss requires $2^{1/\gamma}$
times the compute: 49 times under the Chinchilla refit with $\kappa=1$ ($\gamma=0.178$) and 161 times under Meta's Llama~3 IsoFLOP
frontier ($\gamma=0.136$), or 2.4 to 3.1 years of frontier compute growth. Integrated-assessment models such as GATE
\citep{erdil2025gate} map compute into automation through capability thresholds rather than loss, so these numbers
discipline only their input side.
