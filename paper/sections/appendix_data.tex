\section{Data Construction}\label{app:data}
\setlength{\emergencystretch}{2em}% allow looser lines in the online appendix

This appendix documents the samples summarized in Table~\ref{tab:app-data}: their sources, the conventions we impose,
the corrections we make to source data, and the sample restrictions. Table~\ref{tab:designs} in Section~\ref{sec:tech}
reports the designs that identify the technology. A single script per module regenerates every sample from the raw
files with fixed seeds, and the replication package lists the download commands. The snapshots of living databases are
from September 23 and 24, 2026.

\subsection{Public Sweeps}\label{app:sweeps}

\paragraph{Common conventions.}
For every sweep we build a run-level panel with the fields $(N,D,L)$, a cluster identifier and the derived
quantities $M=D/N$ and $C=6ND$. We use $C=6ND$ even when a sweep reports a more detailed FLOP count, so that compute
means the same thing across sweeps; the model-free estimator, which needs exact isocosts, is the exception
(Section~\ref{sec:tech}). The sweeps do not share a parameter-count convention, a tokenizer or an output measure
(Table~\ref{tab:app_conventions}). We keep each sweep's own conventions and compare only unit-free objects across
sweeps. The exponents $\alpha$ and $\beta$ and the derived $a$, $\gamma$ and $\sigma^*$ are invariant to the unit of loss.
They are not invariant to the parameter-count convention, which Section~\ref{sec:tech:measurement} treats as a
measurement problem. The asymptote $E$, the levels $A$ and $B$, and the compute-optimal ratio $M^{*}$ are not comparable
across rows.

\input{tables/tableB1_conventions}

\paragraph{Design statistic.}
The off-path spread in Table~\ref{tab:app-data} is the residual standard deviation of an OLS regression of $\ln M$ on
$\ln(6ND)$ over the estimation sample. Figure~\ref{fig:app_designs} plots six public designs with run-level losses
against their isocost lines. Farseer is a near-factorial grid in $(\ln N,\ln D)$ with $\mathrm{corr}(\ln N,\ln
D)=-0.04$. The Chinchilla runs combine nine IsoFLOP profiles with off-profile streaks at fixed model size. Gadre et al.\
and the OLMo ladder are grids in size and token multiplier. DataDecide's final checkpoints all lie at
$D\approx85$--$110\,N$, so only the intermediate checkpoints spread the design.

\paragraph{Farseer.}
The file \texttt{1222\_full.csv} of \citet{li2025predictableb} contains 404 runs, the released single-recipe subset of
the roughly 1,000 models the authors trained: 25 model sizes, each trained on up to 19 token budgets with one data
recipe and with hyperparameters set by the Step Law rules. $N$ excludes both of the model's untied 65,536-row embedding
matrices; we verified that the reported $N$ including embeddings exceeds $N$ by exactly $131{,}072\,h$ for hidden size
$h$. The output is English bits per character on the authors' validation set (the average of its web, paper and book
subsets), which is the output they fit. Three quirks required care. The column labeled \texttt{D/N} takes five values
unrelated to $D/N$, so we recompute $M$ from $D$ and $N$ (range 0.31--2,570). The file's compute column includes
attention FLOPs, at 6.6--8.5 FLOP per parameter per token, and we use $6ND$ instead. Four $(N,D)$ pairs were run twice,
and we keep both copies.

\paragraph{Gadre et al.}
The 104 runs of \citet{gadre2024language} are 34 on C4, 35 on RedPajama and 35 on RefinedWeb. $N$ includes embeddings,
and $D=20\times\text{multiplier}\times N$ exactly. Our main output is the C4 validation loss (174 million tokens), on
which every model is evaluated, because tests of neutral data quality need a common output. In-distribution Paloma
losses serve as a robustness check. The authors' token-level confidence intervals imply a sampling standard error of
$\ln L$ of only 0.0016. The residual standard deviation of a fitted Chinchilla form is 0.023--0.030, so the residuals
reflect misspecification rather than evaluation noise.

\paragraph{OLMo ladder.}
We take the final logged step of 30 runs: five sizes trained to 0.5, 1, 2, 5 and 10 times the Chinchilla token budget,
plus five reruns \citep{bhagia2024establishing}. $N$ is OLMo's count, which excludes only the input embedding; we
verified it against the ladder code. $D$ is the logged token count at the final step. The output is C4-en validation
cross-entropy, and task bits per byte serve as a robustness output. Reruns are clustered with their originals.

\paragraph{Muennighoff et al.}
We parse 229 final losses from the authors' notebook with their own code. The aggregated third-party file mislabels
the tokens-per-epoch and epoch columns, so we rebuild $N$, $D$ and the unique tokens $U$ from the model names. The
primary sample is the 33 single-epoch runs ($D=U$). A robustness variant drops two runs with $D/N<0.4$, following
Besiroglu et al.'s rule for Chinchilla. The parameter-count convention in the authors' map is not documented.

\paragraph{DataDecide.}
The DataDecide release \citep{magnusson2025datadecide} crosses 25 data recipes with 14 model sizes (4M to 1.2B
parameters, excluding the input embedding) and three seeds per size (five at 1B), for 1,100 runs. $D$ is
step$\times$batch$\times$2,048, using the batch sizes in the authors' code, and the output is C4-en validation
cross-entropy. We keep 21,888 checkpoints with $D/N\ge5$ that lie at or before the end of the full schedule. The final
checkpoints have $D\approx85$--$110\,N$, and even with checkpoints $\mathrm{corr}(\ln N,\ln D)=0.91$. The checkpoints
that break the collinearity are evaluated before the cosine schedule has finished, and schedule progress is itself a
function of $D/N$. The levels of the fitted technology are therefore not interpretable, and we use DataDecide only for
comparisons across recipes, which share sizes, steps and schedules. This rests on the assumption that the unfinished
schedule affects all recipes alike; for the same reason DataDecide is excluded ex ante from the technologies of
Section~\ref{sec:wedge}.

\paragraph{Sweeps we did not use.}
The Hugging Face release of \citet{kricheli2026tokens} contains no validation losses, only per-epoch training losses
from small multi-epoch runs, so we report no estimates from it. We did not process the open-sci-ref collection of
reference runs on eight corpora, which would allow a second multi-recipe neutrality test.

\subsection{Chinchilla Extraction}\label{app:chinchilla}

The Epoch AI digitization of Figure~4 of \citet{hoffmann2022training} contains 245 runs \citep{besiroglu2024chinchilla},
with training compute from $1.4\times10^{18}$ to $1.3\times10^{22}$ FLOP and model sizes from 57 million to 16 billion
parameters. Hoffmann et al.'s list of model sizes starts at 44 million parameters; that size is not among the digitized
runs, rather than among the dropped ones. $N$ is the total parameter count and $C$ is read off the figure's horizontal
axis, so $D=C/(6N)$ is constructed. Two consequences follow. Digitization error in $C$ passes one for one into $\ln D$ and
is correlated with $\ln C$, an errors-in-variables structure that matters mainly for the separation of $E$ from the
outer exponent. And if the budgets in the figure count FLOPs differently from $6ND$, for example with embedding and
attention terms whose share varies with size, the constructed $D$ inherits an error correlated with $N$; Online
Appendix~\ref{app:add-modelfree} bounds its effect on $\sigma^*$, and we have not rebuilt $D$ from Hoffmann et al.'s
architecture table. The loss is MassiveText validation loss in nats per token, read from a 256-color map and hence
quantized to about $\pm0.01$. Besiroglu et al.\ drop the five highest-loss runs, all from the $10^{19}$ IsoFLOP budget and
with 2.0 to 6.8 billion parameters, and we do the same ($n=240$). Four of the five have $D/N<0.4$; the fifth has 0.40.
Online Appendix~\ref{app:add-chinchilla} shows how the results change when they are kept.

Only part of the extraction consists of IsoFLOP profiles. Digitized $\log_{10}C$ sits systematically 0.018 below the
nine nominal budgets, $6\times10^{18}$ to $3\times10^{21}$ FLOP. We estimate this offset as the median deviation of runs
within 0.1 of a budget and remove it. A run belongs to an IsoFLOP profile if its corrected $\log_{10}C$ is within 0.045 of
a budget. This rule gives 137 profile runs of 245 (132 of 240). The other 108 runs form 35 streaks of fixed model size,
the training-horizon runs that Hoffmann et al.\ pool into their Approach~3. In the 240-run sample, 33 percent of the
variance of centered $(\ln N,\ln D)$ lies across the expansion path: 37 percent among the profile runs and 21 percent
among the off-profile runs. Assigning every run to its nearest budget, as a nine-cluster bootstrap requires, puts
off-profile runs from a $10^{22}$-FLOP trunk into the $3\times10^{21}$ IsoFLOP experiment. Our cluster schemes therefore
group profile runs by budget and off-profile runs by their model-size trunk, or treat the latter as singletons
(Table~\ref{tab:app_conventions}); Online Appendix~\ref{app:add-chinchilla} compares all of them. The 123-point Chinchilla series in the open IsoFLOP
compilation of \citet{czech2026problems} is a snapped subset of the same digitization, on eight budgets, rather than an
independent source; we use it only to cross-check the IsoFLOP path in Online Appendix~\ref{app:wedge-labown}.

\subsection{Laboratory IsoFLOP Profiles and Hyperparameter Sweeps}\label{app:isoflop}

\paragraph{Llama 3.}
The 133 points of Meta's IsoFLOP figure \citep{grattafiori2024llama} come from the digitization of
\citet{czech2026llama3isoflop}, distributed in the compilation of \citet{czech2026problems}; we checked that the two
agree row by row. There are ten budgets from $6\times10^{18}$ to $10^{22}$ FLOP. Two of them, $3\times10^{21}$ and
$10^{22}$ FLOP, have six runs each and do not bracket their minimum with two runs on each side; the model-free estimator
drops them, and Online Appendix~\ref{app:wedge-labown} shows what they do to Meta's path. $D$ is read off the figure
and $N=C/(6D)$ uses the nominal budget, which assumes that Meta's budgets are $6ND$. The loss units are not stated; the
losses lie between 0.69 and 0.93. Parametric levels from these runs are therefore not comparable with other sweeps, but
the model-free estimator and the wedge are invariant to monotone transformations of the output.

\paragraph{Marin.}
The Marin project's scaling ladders \citep{marin2026ladders} train a Llama-2-style architecture on three corpora
(Comma, DCLM and Nemotron-CC): 85, 85 and 88 runs on seven or eight budgets from $1.8\times10^{18}$ to
$3\times10^{20}$ FLOP. The output is the macro-averaged Paloma loss. The nominal budgets are three times the forward
FLOPs and differ from $6ND$ with the configuration parameter count by $-7$ to $+35$ percent. The difference is strongly
negatively correlated with $N$ within a budget, so FLOP accounting, as well as the finite IsoFLOP grid, affects
IsoFLOP-parabola estimates on these data. The model-free estimates use $N=C_b/(6D)$, which makes each profile an exact
isocost; with the configuration count they are 0.04 to 0.07 lower. We also estimate the checkpoint-interpolated
FineWeb/C4 profiles of \citet{li2025misfitting} (176 points) for the duality tests of Online
Appendix~\ref{app:add-chinchilla} and the allocative comparisons of Online Appendix~\ref{app:obs}; because their points come before the learning-rate schedule ends, they are excluded
from the technologies of Section~\ref{sec:wedge}.

\paragraph{Porian et al.}
The released results of \citet{porian2024resolving} contain 975 runs (772 on RefinedWeb, 203 on OpenWebText2): 264
base, 61 tuned, 566 hyperparameter-sweep and 84 seed runs of 16 architectures (width 96--1,504, depth 3--30, vocabulary
50,432, sequence length 2,048). We count parameters and FLOPs without the output head (Kaplan), with it (standard) and
with attention FLOPs (attention), as the authors do, and add a fourth count that also includes the input and
positional embeddings (total). Our re-implementation of the authors' IsoFLOP pipeline reads each run's loss along its
loss curve at twelve budgets $1.25\times10^{16}\times2^{i}$ ($i=0,\dots,11$), takes the minimum over runs for each $N$,
locates the minimizing $N$ by spline interpolation, and reproduces the published path estimates to within 0.003. The
first step of their decomposition uses training loss and the remaining steps validation loss. All runs use a constant
learning rate without cooldown, so the profiles are losses of unfinished schedules.

\paragraph{Step Law.}
The dense-model file of \citet{li2025predictablea} contains 1,911 runs in 17 $(N,D)$ cells, the released subset of the
roughly 3,700 models the authors trained. $N$ takes five values between 215M and 1.07B non-embedding parameters, $D$
ranges from 4B to 100B tokens, and $M$ ranges from 19 to 466. Each cell has a grid of up to 12 learning rates and 10
batch sizes; one cell has only 5 learning rates and 47 runs. Batch size is in sequences of 2,048 tokens, and the output
is the smoothed final training loss of a single-epoch run. We classify 9.5 percent of runs as diverged: those whose log
loss exceeds the cell minimum by more than 0.30.

\subsection{The Controlled Experiment}\label{app:sweep}

\paragraph{Corpora and tokenizer.}
The two corpora are FineWeb-Edu and FineWeb \citep{penedo2024fineweb}; FineWeb-Edu keeps the documents of the same web
crawl that an educational-quality classifier rates highly. We use the first two files of each sample-10BT release for
the main grid. The first 20,000 documents of each corpus are held out for validation (24.6 million FineWeb-Edu tokens
and 16.7 million FineWeb tokens). The remaining 1.76 billion and 1.70 billion training tokens exceed the largest
main-grid budget (800 million), so no run repeats data. The high-$M$ runs append two further files of each release
(1.78 billion and 1.72 billion tokens) after the original permutation, so they also see every token once. A byte-level
BPE tokenizer \citep{sennrich2016neural} with 8,192 tokens, including an end-of-text token that separates documents, is
trained on about 300 million characters of each training corpus. Because both corpora share the tokenizer, losses are
in the same units. On the validation sets a token covers 3.89 bytes of FineWeb-Edu and 3.74 bytes of FineWeb. We use
these ratios to convert losses to bits per byte, $L/(3.89\ln2)$ and $L/(3.74\ln2)$, which removes the difference in how
well the shared tokenizer compresses the two corpora.

\paragraph{Architecture and input counts.}
The models are decoder-only transformers with RMSNorm pre-normalization \citep{zhang2019root}, rotary position
embeddings \citep{su2024roformer}, bias-free linear layers, a four-times-wide GELU feed-forward block and an input
embedding tied to the output projection. Width $d_{\rm model}$ determines the number of layers and of heads, both
$d_{\rm model}/64$, so the head dimension is always 64. Table~\ref{tab:app_sweepdesign} lists the shapes. We record
both parameter counts. The non-embedding count is $n_{\rm layer}(12d_{\rm model}^2+2d_{\rm model})+d_{\rm model}$, and
the tied embedding adds $8{,}192\,d_{\rm model}$, which is 73 percent of all parameters at $d_{\rm model}=128$ and 10
percent at 640. This range makes the counting convention matter far more than in production models, which is the
input-measurement problem of \citet{pearce2024reconciling} and \citet{porian2024resolving}. Training FLOP per token,
including attention and the output projection, is within 7 percent of $6N_{\rm total}$ at every shape but 1.14 to 3.83
times $6N_{\rm ne}$. Initialization is Gaussian with standard deviation $1/\sqrt{\text{fan-in}}$. Residual-branch output
projections are scaled down by $1/\sqrt{2n_{\rm layer}}$, and the embedding has standard deviation 0.02.

\paragraph{Optimization and schedule.}
All runs use AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019decoupled} with decay rates 0.9 and 0.99 for the first and second moments,
stability constant $10^{-8}$, decoupled weight decay 0.1 and gradient clipping at norm 1. The sequence length is 256
and the batch is 64 sequences (16,384 tokens). The learning rate warms up linearly over 250 steps (4.1 million tokens)
to its peak and then stays constant on the trunk. For each budget $D_k$ the run takes $S_k=\lceil D_k/16{,}384\rceil$
steps. At step $0.8S_k$ the model and optimizer state are copied, and the copy is trained to $S_k$ with learning rate
$\mathrm{lr}^{*}\bigl(1-\sqrt{(s-0.8S_k)/(0.2S_k)}\bigr)$. The largest budget is the trunk's own cooldown. This
warmup-stable-decay design with cooldown branches follows \citet{hagele2024scaling}, who find that it performs on par
with cosine schedules tuned to each budget. One trunk thus delivers a whole column of the $(N,D)$ design, and every
endpoint is annealed. Endpoints of one shape share the trunk up to their branch points, so their noise is positively
correlated across budgets, and inference clusters by width.

\paragraph{Learning rate.}
Before the main grid, we trained widths 128, 320 and 512 at four peak learning rates ($1$, $2$, $4$ and
$8\times10^{-3}$) for 50 million FineWeb-Edu tokens and evaluated them on FineWeb-Edu. For each width, the optimal rate
is the minimum of a quadratic in the log learning rate, allowed to extrapolate at most 0.35 log points beyond the tested
range. The optima are $5.75\times10^{-3}$, $2.47\times10^{-3}$ and $1.66\times10^{-3}$. A log-linear fit gives
$\mathrm{lr}^{*}(d_{\rm model})=3.07\times10^{-3}(d_{\rm model}/256)^{-0.90}$, which we apply to both corpora and every
budget. The learning rate is thus a flexible input optimized over width at one budget and on one corpus. By the argument
of Online Appendix~\ref{app:add-measurement}, any remaining inefficiency that varies with $D$ or with the corpus would
bias the data exponent or the measured factor bias. Two additions measure it: the same calibration repeated on FineWeb,
and runs at half and twice the rule's learning rate at width 128 over the full budget ladder and at width 640 at 25 and
50 million tokens, on both corpora. The batch size is the same at every width.

\paragraph{High-$M$ runs.}
In the main grid, ratios above 600 are reached only by the two- and three-layer models. Four-layer models of width 128
(both corpora) and 256 (FineWeb-Edu) are therefore trained to budgets of 200 million, 800 million, 1.6 billion and 3.2
billion tokens, which raises the largest $M$ to 4,063 in non-embedding and 1,743 in total parameters.

\paragraph{Common random numbers, seeds and evaluation.}
Within a corpus, every shape in the main grid uses initialization seed 0 and the same permutation of training
sequences, so differences across $(N,D)$ cells are not driven by different data draws. The shared seed does not create
common random numbers in the weights, because the shapes differ; only the data order is common. The two corpora
necessarily differ in data. Seed replicates rerun widths 128, 256 and 384 with two further seeds, each changing both the
initialization and the data order, to budgets of 50, 100 and 200 million tokens in both corpora, and width 128 with one
further seed to 200, 400 and 800 million tokens, the high-$M$ corner. With the main grid they give three replicates per
cell at low $M$ and two at the corner, which measure the variance of pure noise in the output. Every endpoint is
evaluated on the first 1,048,576 tokens (4,096 sequences of 256) of both validation sets, so each corpus's models have an
in-distribution and a cross-distribution loss and the corpora can be compared on a common output. Most added runs are
also evaluated on the validation and test sets of WikiText-103 \citep{merity2016pointer}, 655 thousand tokens drawn from
neither corpus. For each endpoint we record $N$ under both conventions, the tokens trained, compute under three
conventions, all validation losses and the trunk's training loss. Training curves of the constant-learning-rate trunks
are logged every 1,000 steps.

\paragraph{Pre-registration.}
The pre-analysis plan (\texttt{paper/notes/m9\_preanalysis\_plan.md}) was committed to the project's version-control
repository at 03:12 (UTC+3) on 24 September 2026 (commit \texttt{bd5c0ad}); its header gives 03:15, the time of
writing. At that moment part of the FineWeb-Edu main grid had finished and its raw endpoint losses had appeared in run
logs, but no model had been fitted and no statistic computed; the FineWeb grid, the additions above and the high-$M$ runs
had not started. The plan fixes the output (bits per byte; a corpus effect counts as quality only if it has the same sign
on the other corpus's validation set and on WikiText-103), both parameter conventions with the non-embedding count
primary for the extrapolation test, the robustness samples (without the 25-million-token budget and without the two- and
three-layer shapes), the estimators, the inference (a wild cluster bootstrap by width and a parametric bootstrap with the
within-trunk covariance from the seed replicates), six questions with decision rules, a power calculation to be computed
before any estimate, and the commitment to report every answer. One deviation was made for time after the plan was
committed: the plan called for both four-layer shapes on both corpora and for continuing the two-layer width-128 model
to 3.2 billion tokens, but FineWeb trains only the width-128 four-layer shape and the continuation was dropped.

\paragraph{Hardware.}
All models were trained in MLX \citep{hannun2023mlx} on a single Apple M5 Max. Parameters and optimizer moments are
kept in 32-bit floating point, and every matrix product is computed in bfloat16 by casting the weights on the fly, so
that small updates late in the cooldown are not lost to rounding. \textcolor{red}{[TBD-m9: completed endpoints by corpus

\subsection{Released Models}\label{app:choices}

\paragraph{The Epoch universe.}
We start from the 2,070 language-model rows of the Epoch AI database \citep{epochai2026data} (snapshot of September
23, 2026) and exclude 1,728 of them: derived models such as fine-tunes and post-training updates (531); rows without
$N$ (361) or released outside January 2019--September 2026 (234); rows whose token count cannot be recovered (224);
multimodal and non-text models, whose $D$ would include other tokens (133); benchmark-scale research models with unique
corpora below $5\times10^{9}$ tokens (100); encoders and encoder-decoders (58); post-trained variants identified by name
(16); mixture-of-experts models without an active-parameter count (27); rows with implausible epochs ($>20$) or
$D/N>30{,}000$ (15); and 29 manual exclusions with recorded reasons, such as post-training updates listed as base models
(Llama~3.3 70B), systems-paper benchmark runs and leaked GPT-4 figures. This leaves 342 models.

$D$ is the dataset size times the number of epochs, with missing epochs set to 1. Epoch records some older datasets in
words. Where Epoch's notes indicate a word count, we convert it to tokens, using operation-counted compute when it is
informative and a ratio of 4/3 tokens per word otherwise (7 rows). Where the dataset size is missing but compute is
operation-counted and at least ``likely'', we set $D=C/(6N)$ (67 rows); such rows inherit any error in Epoch's compute
and enter the conduct tests only if Epoch rates them ``Confident''. Six token counts are corrected from primary
sources, each documented in Epoch's own notes. GPT-3 175B was trained on 300B tokens, and GLM-130B on 400B rather than
152B. For Cerebras-GPT-13B (257B) and BloombergGPT (569B), Epoch's dataset size already counts tokens seen.
Polyglot-Ko-12.8B saw 167B tokens rather than 96B words, and for GPT-SW3 3.5B we use 102B tokens. For
mixture-of-experts models, active parameters come from the name, Epoch's notes, or operation-counted compute. Eleven
production-scale rows still have $D$ inconsistent with Epoch's operation-counted or reported compute
($|\ln C/(6ND)|>\ln1.5$); they are flagged.

\paragraph{The verified sample.}
The verified sample starts from the 148 base models in the ObsScaling evaluation file \citep{ruan2024observational},
with nine documented corrections and fills. For example, OPT's $D$ is 300B tokens, not the 180B-token corpus, and
TinyLlama v1.1 was trained on 2T tokens, not 3T. We drop 17 models whose token counts are undisclosed (Mistral~7B and
NeMo, Mixtral, Jamba, DeciLM, Qwen1.5-110B, GPT-2) or ambiguous (InternLM2 7B and 20B), upcycled, duplicated or
instruction-tuned. We replace the deduplicated Pythia suite with the standard suite \citep{biderman2023pythia}. We then
add 57 models released in 2024--2026, whose token counts come from model cards or technical reports. For 47 of them, a
regular expression matches the training-token statement in the saved model card. This gives 188 base models in 73
families. Excluding 15 code-specialized models gives the 173 general-purpose models, in 68 families (67 when Llama~3
and~3.1 are merged into one herd), used in Section~\ref{sec:wedge}. The ObsScaling file lists models on a public
leaderboard, which selects on popularity; this matters for tests whose outcome is usage, not for the wedge itself.

For 143 of the 188 models, $N$ is the exact count of floating-point tensors in the Hugging Face checkpoint. Integer
buffers such as stored causal masks are excluded; they inflate the count of the smallest GPT-NeoX models by up to 36
percent. The 417M-parameter vision encoder of the multimodal Gemma~3 checkpoints is subtracted. For the remaining
models the reported size is used. Mixture-of-experts models enter with active parameters, for example 37B of 671B for
DeepSeek-V3, and with total parameters in the bounds of Online Appendix~\ref{app:wedge-conv}. Embedding counts, from
each model's configuration file, give the non-embedding $N$ that the Farseer and other non-embedding technologies
require; for MPT-7B and~30B, BTLM-3B and Falcon-180B, whose configuration files require authentication, we use the
published architecture. Of the 119 verified models that also appear in Epoch, 5 have token counts that differ from
Epoch's by more than 0.05 in $\log_{10}$. All five differences are documented; Phi-2, for instance, saw 1.4T tokens,
whereas Epoch lists the 250B-token dataset. The release date is Epoch's publication date when matched and otherwise the
developer's announcement or the Hub upload date.

\paragraph{The clean inference-demand sample.}
Nine steps, defined before any wedge was computed, reduce the verified sample to 77 models from 36 families and 18
developers, released 2023--2025: one observation per pretraining run; no research suites, replications or data
benchmarks whose $D$ was fixed by design; no mixture-of-experts, distilled, pruned or derived-initialization,
multimodal, instruction-only or non-transformer models; and token counts with a primary source. Online
Appendix~\ref{app:wedge-sample} lists every step with its sources. The sample's families are classified by whether all
members share a token budget (within 10 percent), and its developers by whether they served their own language models at
the model's release (Online Appendix~\ref{app:wedge-conduct}).

\paragraph{Production-scale universe.}
Trends and conduct tests use models trained with $6ND\ge10^{21}$ FLOP. This universe applies the same threshold to both
sources. It consists of Epoch rows that are not speculative, not code models and not matched to the verified sample
(176), plus the verified general-purpose models above the threshold (159): 335 models released in 2019--2026, 271 of
them open weight, from 94 developers after we merge name variants (for example, all Alphabet units into Google). Three
subsets enter Section~\ref{sec:wedge}. The test of open against closed models and the trends use the 186 models with
confident parameter and token counts (Epoch's ``Confident'' class or documented by the developer), without
mixture-of-experts or non-transformer architectures, and with the clean-sample exclusions applied to verified models;
141 of them are open weight. The bunching test uses the 220 open-weight models released in 2023--2026, other than
non-transformers, with their total parameter counts.

\paragraph{Usage.}
From the Hugging Face model tree (the \texttt{childrenModelCount} field of the Hub interface) we count, for each clean
model, the quantizations, fine-tunes, adapters and merges derived from its base repository, its official post-trained
releases and its same-run re-releases (74 models; MosaicML's and Cerebras's repositories require authentication). The
per-model token volumes that OpenRouter displays are not available through its documented interface, and its terms of
service prohibit scraping; we use the developer-level totals published by \citet{aubakirova2026state} instead. We also
recorded download counts, OpenRouter listings and LMArena vote counts, which the analysis does not use.

\paragraph{Units.}
Wedges are computed in each technology's own conventions: the total count for the Chinchilla, Gadre et al.\ and
laboratory technologies, the non-embedding count for Farseer, MiniCPM and the non-embedding Chinchilla refit, OLMo's
count for the OLMo ladder, and non-embedding FLOPs per token for DeepSeek's law. $D$ is in each model's own tokens, while
each technology is in its sweep's tokens. Under the reference technology $\partial\ln w/\partial\ln D=0.43$ and
$\partial\ln w/\partial\ln N=-0.42$, so a 20 percent difference in tokenizer efficiency moves $\ln w$ by about 0.08.

\subsection{Benchmark Panel}\label{app:obspanel}

The benchmark panel of Online Appendix~\ref{app:obs} combines the ObsScaling base-model file with the Sloth data
\citep{maiapolo2024sloth}. Sloth adds base models absent from ObsScaling and fills missing scores. Five Sloth rows that
duplicate ObsScaling's deduplicated Pythia scores are dropped. Parameter counts are Hub floating-point tensor counts
where available (17 models were affected by the causal-mask correction), and reported token counts are corrected much
as in the verified sample. The outputs are HellaSwag (10-shot), ARC-Challenge (25-shot) and Winogrande (5-shot)
accuracies from the same evaluation harness. We map accuracy to the log odds of above-chance success,
$q=\operatorname{logit}\bigl((\text{acc}-\text{chance})/(1-\text{chance})\bigr)$, with chance-adjusted accuracy
clipped to $[0.01,0.99]$; the composite is the logit of the mean chance-adjusted accuracy of the three tasks.
The main sample contains 128 dense models with $N$, $D$ and all three outputs, from 38 families and 22 developers,
released March 2021--July 2024. Mixture-of-experts models are excluded. The overlap sample ($N\le14$B, $D\le5$T)
has 90 models. Inside the convex hull of the experimental designs lie 20 models, or 57 if the released OLMo-2 7B and
13B models are counted as benchmark runs (Online Appendix~\ref{app:obs}).

The panel's design is revealing. $C=6ND$ holds in every row by construction. Within families the standard deviation of
$\ln D$ is 0.42, against 1.29 for $\ln N$. Only 18 families (66 models) vary $D$ at all, and
$\mathrm{corr}(\ln N,\ln D)=0.46$. The experimental benchmarks are the OLMo ladder, the 104 Gadre et al.\ models, the
Pythia checkpoints and the final DataDecide checkpoints; the OLMo-2 releases, whose inputs their developer chose, enter
only a robustness design. They were evaluated with their developers' own harnesses, so
the correspondence between output formats is part of the maintained null hypothesis in Online Appendix~\ref{app:obs}.

\subsection{Algorithmic-Progress Data}\label{app:ho}

We re-implement line by line the sample construction of \citet{ho2024algorithmic} from their public notebooks, starting
from the 408-row sheet. We keep rows with numeric $N$ and $D$, not marked as excluded, outliers or uncertain, and with
at least one perplexity. We stack the rows by benchmark, drop four named systems ($\mu$P and LoRA fine-tunes), and keep
the three best-performing models per paper. This yields 231 observations from 144 papers: 103 on WikiText-103, 80 on
Penn Treebank and 48 on WikiText-2. $D$ is the dataset size in unique tokens, not tokens processed. Epochs are known for
155 of the 231 rows; we impute the rest by the median within bins of $\log_{10}D$, and set epochs to one for
$D\ge10^{10}$. The effective-data variant follows \citet{muennighoff2023scaling}. The output is log perplexity, mostly
per word; its units vary with each evaluation's tokenization.

For the allocative-efficiency analysis we build a separate sample of Epoch language models
\citep{epochai2026data}. Of the 792 models with $N$ and $C$, 544 report a dataset size and 454 are not fine-tunes. Of
these, 428 are dense. In 339 of them, $C/(6N)$ lies within a factor of three of dataset size times epochs, and 247 of
those were trained for at most four epochs. We set $D=C/(6N)$ so that $6ND=C$ exactly.
