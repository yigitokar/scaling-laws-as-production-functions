\section[Monte Carlo Design and Results]{Appendix \thesection. Monte Carlo Design and Results}\label{app:mc}
\setlength{\emergencystretch}{2em}% allow looser lines in the online appendix

This appendix documents the Monte Carlo design behind Section~\ref{sec:ident}: what alternative experimental designs
identify when they use the same total compute. The truth is the technology of \citet{besiroglu2024chinchilla}:
$E=1.8172$, $A=482.01$, $B=2085.43$, $\alpha=0.3478$ and $\beta=0.3658$, so that $a=0.513$, $\gamma=0.178$,
$\sigma^*=0.737$ and $\ln M^*(10^{24})=2.898$ ($M^*=18.1$). ``Bias'' is the Monte Carlo mean error and ``RMSE'' the
root-mean-squared error around the truth. No estimator is started at the true parameters.

\textit{Design.} Every design has 90 runs and the same total compute, $5.1\times10^{22}$ FLOP, that of ten runs on each of
Chinchilla's nine nominal IsoFLOP budgets ($6\times10^{18}$ to $3\times10^{21}$ FLOP). All designs except the factorial grid
place ten runs on each of these budgets; the factorial grid crosses nine values of $N$ with ten of $D$, each spanning a
factor of 256 around the expansion path and scaled to the same total compute, so that its runs sit at 89 compute levels
from $1.8\times10^{17}$ to $1.2\times10^{22}$ FLOP. Apart from that benchmark, the designs differ only in where the runs
sit on their isocost lines (Table~\ref{tab:app-mc-designs}): near the expansion path, as in a
compute-optimal ladder; spread by IsoFLOP profiles or by a factorial grid; spread by allocation errors with standard
deviation $\varsigma$ in $\ln(D/N)$ at given compute, as by optimizing labs that err; or tilted by labs that follow
Kaplan's allocation rule. Log loss carries normal noise with standard deviation 0.0075, about the residual standard
deviation (0.0076) of the Besiroglu fit on the 240-run Chinchilla sample, an upper value for pure seed noise. Three estimators are
compared. The primal is the Huber ($\delta=10^{-3}$) fit of $\ln L$ with $\kappa=1$
\citep{hoffmann2022training,besiroglu2024chinchilla}, from nine starting values drawn at random within the region of Hoffmann et al.'s
initialization grid ($\ln A,\ln B\in[0,25]$, $\ln E\in[-1,1]$, $\alpha,\beta\in[0.1,1.2]$). The dual reads $a$ from the optima (or IsoFLOP minima) and $\gamma$ from the frontier and recovers
$\alpha=\gamma/a$ and $\beta=\gamma/(1-a)$, which uses optimality and $\kappa=1$. The third is a profile likelihood for
$\sigma^*$ with $\kappa$ free, on a 29-point grid over $[0.05,0.99]$, started at each grid point from the members of the
observationally equivalent family of Proposition~\ref{prop:fd}(iv) that reproduce the primal and dual fits.

\textit{On-path data do not identify the technology through the primal estimator.} When every run is exactly
compute-optimal ($\varsigma=0$), 87 percent of fits end on a corner, with one power-law term switched off, and the RMSE of
$\hat\sigma^*$ is 0.27, against 0.004 for an IsoFLOP design spanning $\pm16\times$ the optimal size and 0.005 for the
factorial grid. No corner fit has a higher objective than the true parameters, so the data are fitted as well by a
technology with one input switched off as by the truth. Near the path the failure is a ridge: at $\varsigma=0.02$ the
median error of $\hat\sigma^*$ is $-0.12$ and the 5--95 percent range of $\hat\sigma^*$ is $[0.34,0.75]$, and the normalized condition
number of the information matrix rises from $1.4\times10^{3}$ to $4.0\times10^{3}$ in the designed experiments to
$1.5\times10^{7}$. As
$\varsigma$ rises to 0.05, 0.1 and 0.2, the RMSE of $\hat\sigma^*$ falls to 0.089, 0.025 and 0.015. The compute-optimal
ratio is more demanding once optimality is not imposed: the RMSE of $\ln\hat M^*(10^{24})$ is 28 at $\varsigma=0$, 4.6 at
0.1, 2.0 at 0.3 and 0.59 at 1, against 0.16 under the IsoFLOP design. The RMSE of $\ln\hat M^*$ falls roughly as $1/\varsigma$, and the
$\kappa$-free profile sets below narrow roughly as $1/\varsigma^2$ (median widths 0.104 at $\varsigma=1$ and 0.028 at
$\varsigma=2$): these are the finite-sample counterparts of the information rates of Proposition~\ref{prop:A-info}. With
$\kappa=1$ imposed, the RMSE of $\hat\sigma^*$ instead flattens, for the reason given next.

\textit{With $\kappa=1$ imposed, apparent precision at intermediate $\varsigma$ is functional form.} For
$0.1\le\varsigma\le0.3$ the primal RMSE of $\hat\sigma^*$ (0.013 to 0.025) is no better than that of the dual estimator
(0.012), which uses only the path: with $\kappa=1$, $\alpha+\beta=\gamma/[a(1-a)]$, and $a(1-a)$ is flat near $a=\tfrac12$,
so $\sigma^*\approx2/(2+4\gamma)$ is pinned down by the frontier elasticity. Freeing $\kappa$ removes that information
(Table~\ref{tab:app-mc-designs}, Panel~B). The 95 percent profile set for $\sigma^*$ is the whole grid in 91 percent of
on-path replications and 95 percent at $\varsigma=0$. Larger allocation errors first rule out poor substitutes: at
$\varsigma=0.3$ the median bounds are $[0.42,0.99]$, and the set contains every $\sigma^*\ge0.9$ in 76 percent of
replications. The asymmetry follows from the Harberger triangle of Lemma~\ref{lem:ce}: an error in $\ln M$ costs a
Farrell loss proportional to $(1-\sigma^*)/\sigma^*$, which vanishes as $\sigma^*\to1$. The set becomes informative at
$\varsigma=1$, with median bounds of $[0.69,0.79]$. In the designed experiments it is tight (median bounds 0.729 and 0.745
for IsoFLOP $\pm16\times$), and it covers the truth in 93 to 100 percent of replications in every design with independent
noise. The dual estimator is precise on the path
(RMSE 0.011 for $\sigma^*$) but inherits any systematic allocation error: when labs optimize against Kaplan's beliefs
($N\propto C^{0.73}$) it is biased by $-3.92$ in $\ln M^*$, understating $M^*$ about 50-fold.

\textit{Inference, starting values and noise.} On the path the Wald covariance is numerically singular, or the fit is at
a corner, in 79 percent of replications, and the pairs bootstrap with every draw warm-started at the full-sample estimate
covers $\sigma^*$ in 44 percent of 150 replications; restarting every draw also from the nine starting values raises coverage
from 33 to 78 percent in a separate check, with a median interval width of 0.43 against 0.016 under the IsoFLOP design.
With designed variation, Wald and bootstrap coverage is 94 to 97 percent. Starting values matter only near the path: with
64 random starts the corner shares on the path and at $\varsigma=0$ fall from 72 and 87 percent to 18 and 56 percent, with
RMSEs of $\hat\sigma^*$ of 0.19 and 0.30; for $\varsigma\ge0.1$ and the designed experiments the start set
makes no difference. The RMSE of $\hat\sigma^*$ under the IsoFLOP design is 0.0024, 0.0036 and 0.0078 at noise standard
deviations of 0.005, 0.0075 and 0.015. With heteroskedastic noise (standard deviation proportional to $(ND)^{-1/4}$) and
half of each run's variance shared within its budget or training trunk, the ranking of designs is unchanged (RMSE 0.0042
for IsoFLOP, 0.0052 for the factorial grid, 0.022 at $\varsigma=0.3$ and 0.25 on the path), but Wald intervals that assume
independence cover $\sigma^*$ in only 82, 74 and 61 percent of replications under the IsoFLOP, factorial and
$\varsigma=0.3$ designs, and profile sets calibrated as if the noise were independent cover it in 71 to 92 percent. Inference must allow for dependence within budgets or training trunks, as the analysis of our
experiment does (Online Appendix~\ref{app:sweep}). The seed standard deviation of $\ln L$ measured in the
experiment is \textcolor{red}{[TBD-m9: seed standard deviation of $\ln L$]}, against the noise standard deviations of

\textit{A simulated industry.} A second design simulates forty labs that choose compute and allocations over six model
generations and asks which production-function estimators recover the technology from released models; it is reported
in the companion paper \citep{okar2026observational}. One result bears on Section~\ref{sec:wedge}: stacking the loss equation with the training-only
first-order condition ($w=1$) biases $\ln M^*$ by $+1.25$ to $+1.28$ under every compute rule, close to the closed form
$2\,\E[\ln w]/(\alpha+\beta)=1.28$, because the first-order-condition residual is the wedge of
Proposition~\ref{prop:A-wedge}; ignoring it makes over-trained models look like evidence that data are cheap.

\textit{Reproducibility.} The code is in \texttt{code/analysis/m6\_montecarlo} (entry point \texttt{run\_v2.py}). Every
design cell seeds its own random-number generator, so results do not depend on the number of processes; on four CPU
processes Design~A takes 18 minutes.
