\section{What Optimizing Labs' Data Identify}\label{sec:ident}

Section~\ref{sec:framework} described the technology by four objects: the slope $a$ of the expansion path, the
frontier elasticity $\gamma$, the elasticity of substitution at the optimum $\sigma^*$ and the compute-optimal ratio
$M^*(C)$. Reading developers' choices requires the last two: by equation~\eqref{eq:wM}, $M^*(C)$ is the zero point of the
wedge and $\sigma^*$ sets its scale. This section asks which data identify them. Optimization removes the variation that
identifies curvature (subsection~A). Designed IsoFLOP variation identifies $\sigma^*$ without a functional form
(subsection~B). Beyond the largest designed budget, $M^*(C)$ is only partially identified, and for released models what
survives is chiefly the sign of the wedge (subsection~C). Proofs are in Online Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

The results use technology \eqref{eq:tech} with Hicks-neutral productivity only ($\psi_N=\psi_D=0$), and the notation of
Section~\ref{sec:framework}: $a\equiv\beta/(\alpha+\beta)$ and $b\equiv1-a$ are the slopes of the expansion path in
$(\ln N,\ln D)$, $\gamma\equiv\kappa\alpha\beta/(\alpha+\beta)$ is the frontier elasticity, $\sigma^*=2/(2+\alpha+\beta)$,
and $G$ and $K$ are the constants of the path and the frontier (Proposition~\ref{prop:duality}). A sample is
\emph{on-path} if its runs share one technology, each run minimizes training compute for its loss, so that every wedge
equals one, and compute varies over an interval.

\subsection{Optimization Removes the Variation That Identifies Curvature}\label{sec:ident-onpath}

\begin{proposition}[What optimizing labs' data identify, and how fast]\label{prop:ident}
Let runs follow \eqref{eq:tech} with $\psi_N=\psi_D=0$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] (On the path.) In an on-path sample, $n_i=\ln G+a(c_i-\ln6)$ and $d_i=-\ln G+b(c_i-\ln6)$: choices reveal the
path, that is, $a$ and $M^*(C)$, and nothing else. Outcomes satisfy $\E[y\mid c]=\gamma(c-\ln6)-\ln K+\E[\omega\mid c]$, so
within the model $\gamma$ is identified if and only if $\E[\omega\mid c]$ is known up to a constant, as in a single-lab
sweep. Curvature is not identified: for every $\sigma^*\in(0,1)$ some member of family \eqref{eq:tech} generates the same
joint distribution of $(n,d,y)$, and by Lemma~\ref{lemma:sigma} no value $\sigma^*\ge1$ does.
\item[(ii)] (Global versus local identification.) Suppose also that $\E[\omega\mid c]$ is constant and $\kappa=1$.
Globally, $\alpha=\gamma/a$ and $\beta=\gamma/b$ are identified by the functional form, and $A/B$, hence the technology's
own cost-minimizing mix, only by imposing that the observed path is optimal. Locally, identification fails:
perturbations that move the decay rates of the two loss terms along the path, $\alpha a$ and $\beta b$, in opposite
directions leave on-path outcomes unchanged to first order, so the least-squares criterion is of fourth order along
them. The errors of estimators of $\sigma^*$ should then not be expected to shrink faster than the inverse fourth root
of the number of runs, whether or not $\alpha=\beta$, and Wald intervals are invalid. Imposing $\beta/(\alpha+\beta)=a$ restores the
square-root rate.
\item[(iii)] (Off the path.) Let runs deviate from the path with log wedges $\ln w_i$, with $E$ known and Gaussian noise.
To leading order, the information about $\ln M^*$ is proportional to the residual dispersion of the $\ln w_i$. The information about
$\sigma^*$ is bounded by a multiple of $\sum_i\Phi_i^2$, where $\Phi_i\equiv\ln\big(C_i/C_{\min}(R_i)\big)$ is run $i$'s
Farrell allocative loss: the log of its compute relative to the least compute that attains its reducible loss
$R_i=L_i-E$. If the deviations of a fixed design are scaled by $v\to0$, the two informations are $O(v^2)$ and $O(v^4)$,
so the standard error of $\ln\hat M^*$ grows like $v^{-1}$ and that of $\hat\sigma^*$ like $v^{-2}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

A ray has no curvature. Because the FLOP accounting gives every lab the same log-cost weights, cost-minimizing labs that
share a technology place every run on the expansion path, along which the family is quasi-homothetic: reducible loss is
log-linear along the path, and all curvature lies across it. Choices trace the path and outcomes trace the frontier, but
neither reveals how fast loss rises off the path, which is what $\sigma^*$ measures. A one-parameter set of technologies
shares the path and the frontier and differs only off it. At five times the compute-optimal ratio, reducible loss lies
2.0, 4.1 and 7.6 percent above the common frontier for the members with $\sigma^*=0.85$, 0.74 and 0.60 drawn in
Figure~\ref{fig:geometry}, panel~b.

Part (ii) explains why fits of the Chinchilla form to compute-optimal data can nonetheless look precise.
Compute-optimality means that the two loss terms decay at the same rate along the path, so the data see a single
exponential in compute; they cannot tell how its level splits between the terms, and a different split absorbs decay
rates that move apart. The on-path model is thus the analogue of an over-fitted mixture, whose parameters can be learned
only at the fourth-root rate \citep{chen1995optimal}. What precision remains under $\kappa=1$ comes from the restriction
$\beta/(\alpha+\beta)=a$, which ties $\sigma^*=2/(2+\gamma/(ab))$ to the path and the frontier: functional form plus
optimality. Simulations in Online Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} confirm the fourth-root rate, and the square-root rate with
the restriction imposed; the rate rests on such simulations and a heuristic argument, not on a limit theorem
\citep{sargan1983identification,rotnitzky2000likelihood}. The mechanism also reconciles our result with
\citet{kricheli2026tokens}, who find fixed tokens-per-parameter designs ill-conditioned as $|\alpha-\beta|\to0$: the two
terms decay at the same rate along a fixed-ratio ray if and only if $\alpha=\beta$, and along the compute-optimal path
always.

Real ladders and labs deviate from the path. Because the frontier is a power law, output decomposes exactly as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ident-decomp}
y_i \;=\; \gamma\,(c_i-\ln6)-\ln K-\gamma\,\Phi_i-\epsilon_i
\end{equation}
with $\omega$ normalized to zero. Curvature enters output only through the Farrell losses, which are Harberger triangles,
$\Phi_i\approx\sigma^*(\ln w_i)^2/[4(1-\sigma^*)]$. A run reveals where the path lies to the extent that it is off the
path, which is first order in its log wedge, and the path's curvature only to the extent that it wastes compute, which
is second order. Information squares these: halving the dispersion of log wedges quarters the information about $M^*$
and divides the information about $\sigma^*$ by sixteen. The better labs optimize, the less their data reveal.

Proposition~\ref{prop:ident} is the point of \citet{marschak1944random} in its cleanest form: without wedges, every
allocation lies on one ray, and identification requires the variation in relative prices, adjustment frictions or
optimization errors of \citet{bond2005adjustment} \citep[see also][]{gandhi2020identification}. Here these are
heterogeneous wedges from serving demand, data costs, size-dependent prices of a FLOP and binding constraints
(Proposition~\ref{prop:wedge}), and belief errors such as allocation by the \citet{kaplan2020scaling} law. IsoFLOP
sweeps are different: the experimenter assigns the inputs, so their minima estimate the path from outcomes, not from
choices. The rates in part (iii) are the design-theoretic property of estimating a curvature parameter
\citep{box1959design,kiefer1959optimum}; what is specific here is that economic wedges generate the design's spread. The
problem is distinct from the functional dependence of \citet{ackerberg2015identification}, which concerns the first
stage of proxy estimators.

The result binds for compute-optimal ladders, fixed tokens-per-parameter designs run by one lab (DataDecide trains
every size at about 100 tokens per parameter; \citealp{magnusson2025datadecide}) and releases of the compute-optimal
era. Released models since 2023 lie far off the path. Their heterogeneous values of compactness supply transverse
variation, but using it requires the exclusion restriction that these values move the mix without moving the
technology, and the variation may be confounded with factor-biased technology. We therefore take the technology from
designed sweeps and use released models' wedges to read objectives (Section~\ref{sec:wedge}).

The Chinchilla data show part (i) directly. On the 41 runs within 0.15 log points of the fitted compute-optimal size,
the profile likelihood for $\sigma^*$ with $\kappa$ free is flat: its 95 percent confidence set runs from 0.20 to 0.99, the
edge of the grid searched. The full 240-run design, which adds transverse variation, gives $[0.68,0.72]$ (Online
Appendix~\ref{app:additional}).

Figure~\ref{fig:designs} shows what the rates mean at realistic noise and design sizes, in simulated designs of 90 runs
with equal total compute that differ only in where runs sit on their isocost lines. Optimizing labs miss the
compute-optimal $\ln(D/N)$ by a normal error with standard deviation $v$, so their log wedges have standard deviation
$(1/\sigma^*-1)v$. With $v=0$, a Huber fit of the Chinchilla form ends on a corner, with one power-law term
switched off, in 87 percent of replications. As $v$ rises from 0.05 to 0.2, the root mean squared error of
$\hat\sigma^*$ falls from 0.089 to 0.015 (panel A); an IsoFLOP design with the same compute gives 0.0036. The
compute-optimal ratio is harder still: the root mean squared error of $\ln\hat M^*$ is 4.6 at $v=0.1$ and 0.59 at
$v=1$, against 0.16 for the IsoFLOP design.

For moderate allocation errors this precision is functional-form information, as part (ii) predicts. With $\kappa$ free
(panel B), the 95 percent profile confidence set for $\sigma^*$ covers the entire grid searched, $[0.05,0.99]$, in 91
percent of on-path replications. At $v=0.3$ it rules out low elasticities but still contains all of $[0.50,0.95]$ in 65
percent of replications, against widths of 0.016 and 0.019 for the IsoFLOP and factorial designs. Standard inference
also fails on the path: the Wald covariance is singular, or the fit sits on a corner, in 79 percent of on-path
replications, and a bootstrap that restarts every draw from all starting values covers the true $\sigma^*$ only 78
percent of the time. Online Appendix~\ref{app:mc} reports the full design, the sensitivity to starting values, noise
levels and clustered noise, and a simulated industry.

\subsection{Designed Variation Identifies Curvature without a Functional Form}\label{sec:ident-modelfree}

Proposition~\ref{prop:ident} says what a design must supply: movement across the path. The next result reads the
technology off that movement without a functional form. Let $x\equiv\ln M$, so that an IsoFLOP profile is loss as a
function of $x$ at fixed $c$; subscripts denote partial derivatives.

\begin{proposition}[Model-free $\sigma^*$ and $w$]\label{prop:modelfree}
Let $L$ be any measure of output that falls as quality rises (loss, bits per byte, or $\ln(L-E)$ for any $E$), and let
the technology be smooth with positive marginal products.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] At any point, $w=(L_c-L_x)/(L_c+L_x)$.
\item[(ii)] At a compute-optimal point,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ident-mf}
\frac1{\sigma^*}-1 \;=\; \frac{L_{nn}\big|_C}{2\,\lvert dL^*/dc\rvert},
\end{equation}
where $L_{nn}|_C$ is the curvature of the IsoFLOP profile in $\ln N$ at its minimum and $L^*(c)$ is the loss--compute
frontier. The ratio is invariant to strictly monotone transformations of output.
\item[(iii)] At $M^*(C)$, $\partial\ln w/\partial\ln M|_C=1/\sigma^*-1$. In family \eqref{eq:tech}, $\ln w$ is
exactly linear in $\ln(M/M^*(C))$ with this slope; in general the slope varies with $M$.
\item[(iv)] Under $\kappa=1$ the curvature of every IsoFLOP profile is pinned by the slopes of the frontier and the path,
$-y_{nn}|_C=\gamma^2/(ab)$; with $\kappa$ free it is a separate parameter.
\item[(v)] Hence $\sigma^*$ is identified by IsoFLOP profiles together with the frontier slope, and $w$ at a point by local
variation in two non-collinear directions around it, but only inside the design's support.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Part (i) is a change of coordinates: the slopes of any output measure along the isocost and along a ray of constant $M$
give the two marginal products, and their ratio is the wedge. Part (ii) is the elasticity of substitution at the optimum
written in observables. Along an IsoFLOP line, loss changes only through the mix. At the minimum the marginal products of
parameters and tokens are equal; the curvature of the profile measures how fast they diverge as the mix moves, and the
frontier slope, which by the envelope theorem is the marginal product of compute, measures their common level. A profile
that is sharply curved relative to the frontier slope signals poor substitutes. Because both terms are measured in the
same output units, the irreducible loss, the transformation of output and $\kappa$ cancel. Part (iv) shows what $\kappa=1$ does: it ties the curvature of every profile to $(a,\gamma)$, which is the
functional-form information of Proposition~\ref{prop:ident}(ii). Part (iii) gives a test of the family, which
Section~\ref{sec:wedge} applies where a factorial design reaches high $M$.

Both terms of \eqref{eq:ident-mf} are familiar: the quadratic coefficient of the parabola fitted to each IsoFLOP profile,
as in Approach~2 of \citet{hoffmann2022training}, and the slope of the frontier through the minima. Quadratics fitted over
wide grids overstate the curvature slightly \citep{czech2026problems}; narrow windows or quartic fits remove the bias at
some cost in variance (Online Appendix Remark~\ref{rem:A-grid}). Section~\ref{sec:tech} applies the estimator to every
public IsoFLOP design.

\subsection{Beyond the Design: What Remains Identified at Frontier Scale}\label{sec:ident-pi}

Revealed demand needs the wedge at released models, and most were trained with more compute than any design. The largest
public IsoFLOP budget is $10^{22}$ FLOP \citep{grattafiori2024llama}; 72 of the 77 models in the clean sample of
Section~\ref{sec:wedge} used more, and only two lie inside the Chinchilla design's range of both compute and tokens per
parameter. The zero point must therefore be extrapolated. Let $x^*(c)\equiv\ln M^*(C)$, with path slope
$e\equiv d\ln M^*/d\ln C=1-2a$, be identified at the largest design budget $C_J$, and let $c_J\equiv\ln C_J$.

\begin{proposition}[Partial identification beyond the design]\label{prop:pi}
Consider a model with compute $C>C_J$ and ratio $M$, and write $x=\ln M$ and $\Delta c=c-c_J$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] If both factor demands are nondecreasing in compute (normal inputs), then $|e|\le1$ and
$x^*(c)\in[x^*(c_J)-\Delta c,\ x^*(c_J)+\Delta c]$.
\item[(ii)] If $e\in[e_L,e_U]$ beyond the design, the sharp identified set for $x^*(c)$ is
$\mathcal X^*(c)=[x^*(c_J)+e_L\Delta c,\ x^*(c_J)+e_U\Delta c]$; its width grows linearly in the log extrapolation
distance. With sampling error, the anchor's confidence interval replaces $x^*(c_J)$.
\item[(iii)] For any technology with single-peaked IsoFLOP profiles, which $0<\sigma<1$ everywhere guarantees,
$\operatorname{sign}(w-1)=\operatorname{sign}\big(x-x^*(c)\big)$. Hence $w>1$ is identified if and only if
$x>\sup\mathcal X^*(c)$, $w<1$ if and only if $x<\inf\mathcal X^*(c)$, and otherwise the sign is not identified. Neither
$\sigma^*$ nor a functional form is needed.
\item[(iv)] Magnitudes require a range $[k_L,k_U]$ for the slope of $\ln w$ along the isocost, which in family
\eqref{eq:tech} is $1/\sigma^*-1$. If $x>\sup\mathcal X^*(c)$, then
\[
\ln w\in\big[k_L\big(x-\sup\mathcal X^*(c)\big),\ k_U\big(x-\inf\mathcal X^*(c)\big)\big].
\]
\item[(v)] Bounds $w\in[w_L,w_U]$ imply $s\in[1-1/w_L,\,1-1/w_U]$ for the expenditure share $s=(w-1)/w$ and, under
part (ii) of Proposition~\ref{prop:wedge}, $\lambda pT/(3D)\in[w_L-1,\,w_U-1]$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The sign result needs only the location of the minimum. On a single-peaked profile, a model with more tokens per
parameter than is optimal at its compute has a higher marginal product of parameters than of tokens, so $w>1$ whatever
the curvature. Magnitudes need the curvature as well as the distance to the minimum, and the distance is uncertain
because the minimum is extrapolated. At frontier scale the data therefore identify a sign, not a level. With the path
anchored at the largest IsoFLOP budgets (Section~\ref{sec:wedge}), Llama~3 8B, trained on 1,868 tokens per parameter with
$7\times10^{23}$ FLOP, lies above every admissible compute-optimal ratio, so its over-training is identified whatever
$\sigma^*$ is. Llama~3.1 405B, at 38 tokens per parameter and $4\times10^{25}$ FLOP, lies inside the identified set under
every assumption considered there. A confidence interval from one fitted technology, by contrast, reflects only
sampling error within one design and one functional form.

In sum, near-optimal choices reveal the path slope $a$, and a single lab's outcomes the frontier elasticity $\gamma$. The
curvature $\sigma^*$ requires transverse variation but no functional form. The zero point $M^*(C)$ requires optimality
or transverse variation, and beyond the designs it is only partially identified.
