\section{What Over-Training Reveals}\label{sec:wedge}

This section uses the technology of Section~\ref{sec:tech} to read the choices of the developers that release models. Two
findings stand out. With the zero point extrapolated from the designs' own paths and their sampling error, over-training
is identified beyond the designs for most allocation decisions, though not for most training compute; weaker
restrictions on the zero point identify far less (Section~\ref{sec:wedge-pi}). And revealed over-training rose steeply
from 2023 to 2025: the compute-weighted share rose under every technology we consider, though the step from 2024 to
2025 is not statistically established (Section~\ref{sec:wedge-trends}).

\subsection{The Inversion}\label{sec:wedge-measure}

By Proposition~\ref{prop:wedge}, when the price of a training FLOP does not vary with model size, data cost nothing
beyond compute and no cap on tokens binds, $w-1=v_N$, the value of compactness (including the shadow value of any
binding limit on size). In the $\kappa$ family the wedge depends on a model's inputs only through $M=D/N$ and $C=6ND$
(equation~\eqref{eq:wM}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sIV-inv}
\ln w=\Big(\frac1{\sigma^*}-1\Big)\ln\frac{M}{M^*(C)},\qquad
s\equiv\frac{w-1}{w}=1-\Big(\frac{M}{M^*(C)}\Big)^{-(1/\sigma^*-1)}.
\end{equation}
At given compute every technology in the family ranks models by $M$, and in the sample below a regression of
$\ln\hat w$ on $\ln M$ alone has an $R^2$ of 0.9999: what the technology adds to the model cards is the translation into
an economic quantity.

We report the compactness share $s=v_N/(1+v_N)$, which needs no conversion of FLOPs into dollars; what the value consists
of depends on conduct (Section~\ref{sec:framework:wedge}). Released models lie outside every design, so
equation~\eqref{eq:sIV-inv} is an extrapolation; Section~\ref{sec:wedge-pi} checks its direction in $M$ and replaces it
with a sign result in $C$.

\subsection{The Clean Sample and Decision Units}\label{sec:wedge-data}

\textit{Models.} The clean sample starts from the 173 general-purpose open-weight models of the verified sample, whose
parameter and token counts we audited (Online Appendix~\ref{app:data}). Nine steps drop models whose $(N,D)$ is not a
choice of the kind Proposition~\ref{prop:wedge} describes, such as research suites and replications whose $D$ was fixed
by design, mixture-of-experts and distilled models, and token counts without a primary source. The result is 77 dense
models from 18 developers, released between 2023 and 2025 (Online Appendix~\ref{app:wedge-sample}); an earlier version
reported wedges for all 173 under 12 of the 32 technologies below.

\textit{Decision units.} When members of a family share one token budget, their wedges differ only through the
technology (Proposition~\ref{prop:wedge}(iv)), and a budget chosen for them reveals one number, the family share, a
compute-weighted average of member shares. Our unit of analysis is therefore the allocation decision, one choice of
$(N,D)$: members of a family whose budgets are within 10 percent of each other form one decision, and the other models
are decisions of their own, 49 in all. Under a written protocol, checked by a second coder blind to the wedges (Online
Appendix~\ref{app:wedge-family}), we read each common budget in the reports as a family \emph{choice}, a size
\emph{menu}, a \emph{cap} (the developer's whole curated corpus), a cap and a menu, or, where only the corpus size is
stated, \emph{cap or choice}. A cap on tokens, for a family or for a member that repeated its data, makes the share a
lower bound on the value of compactness; under a choice or a menu it is a point estimate, since a binding limit on size
is part of that value (Proposition~\ref{prop:wedge}(iii)). Of the 49 decisions, 29 are point estimates and 20 lower
bounds. Separately, 49 of the 77 models sit in five size windows around quantized memory tiers, where a binding tier's
shadow value is part of the value of compactness. Table~\ref{tab:wedge} flags both.

\textit{Technologies.} A fixed rule admits 32 technologies: every technology estimated on a designed sweep in
Section~\ref{sec:tech}, with $\kappa=1$ and with $\kappa$ free, the path of each IsoFLOP design with its model-free
curvature, the literature's estimates on the Chinchilla data, and the published laws of DeepSeek LLM
\citep{bi2024deepseek} and MiniCPM \citep{hu2024minicpm}. The reference is the Chinchilla technology with $\kappa$ free,
fitted to all runs in total parameters like the models' $M$ ($\sigma^*=0.701$, $M^*(10^{21})=22.7$; 0.700 and 20.6 in
FLOP-effective parameters). Its intervals come from a design-conditional wild bootstrap \citep{feng2011wild}, joint
across the sample, the narrowest of six schemes we compared; we also report a cluster bootstrap that resamples the
Chinchilla runs by IsoFLOP budget and model-size trunk (44 clusters).

Figure~\ref{fig:wedge} and Table~\ref{tab:wedge} report the 49 decision units. Under the reference technology the median
decision has $\hat w=3.8$ and $s=0.73$, with a 95 percent joint wild-bootstrap interval of [0.66, 0.78]: it was made as
if a model one percent smaller at the same quality were worth 2.8 percent of its training cost. The cluster bootstrap
widens it to [0.51, 0.81]. Ninety-eight percent of decisions are over-trained ($\hat w>1$). The unit of analysis hardly
moves the median: it is 0.75 over the 77 models, 0.74 over 56 units that group only whole families on one budget, 0.75
over 66 units that enter members of cap families as lower bounds, and 0.74 over the 29 point-identified decisions.

That interval measures sampling error within one technology; the choice of technology matters more. Across the 32
technologies the median decision share ranges from 0.04 to 0.80, and from 0.17 to 0.80 without MiniCPM's law, whose zero
point lies outside its own design; the $\kappa$-free fits of the large designs and the developers' paths with model-free
curvature give 0.62 to 0.75. All 32 technologies agree that $w>1$ for 28 of the 49 decisions.

\textit{A size-specific family.} SmolLM2 \citep{benallal2025smollm2}, the only family whose three members have their own
budgets and lie outside the tier windows, trained 135 million parameters on 2 trillion tokens, 360 million on 4 trillion
and 1.7 billion on 11 trillion. Under the reference the shares are 0.94, 0.93 and 0.91 (a lower bound: the 1.7B model
trained for about two epochs on its data), and each member's over-training is identified beyond the designs even with a
tilt allowance of 1.84. Hugging Face released the sizes for on-device use and did not serve them, so $s$ is a value of
compactness to users who run the models; trained partly on synthetic data, SmolLM2 has a more secure sign than level.
OLMo~2 \citep{teamolmo2024olmo} is less clean: its 1B and 7B models share a budget, and its 13B and 32B repeated part of
the stage-1 mix (1.2 and 1.5 epochs), a binding cap on unique tokens that makes their shares lower bounds. With $D$
varying 1.5-fold across a 22-fold range of $N$, the fall of its shares with size, from 0.87 to 0.61, is largely the
technology's (Online Appendix~\ref{app:wedge-family}).

\textit{Common budgets.} Meta trained the Llama~3 herd, the 8B and 70B models and the 405B flagship of Llama~3.1, on 15
to 15.6 trillion tokens. The herd reveals one share: 0.29 under the reference, with an interval of [0.08, 0.46], and
0.38 under Meta's own path; the flagship's own sign is not identified beyond the designs. Meta's sources are consistent
with a choice and with a cap: the report describes a curated corpus of about 15 trillion tokens, a flagship sized as
compute-optimal for its budget, and smaller models trained far beyond that point for inference efficiency
\citep{grattafiori2024llama}, and the release post says that the 8B and 70B were still improving when training stopped
\citep{meta2024llama3}. Under a choice 0.29 is the herd's value of compactness; under a cap it is a lower bound. Trained
on one corpus, the herd cannot reveal the contrast Meta describes between a flagship sized for training efficiency and
siblings trained for inference. Across the 17 common budgets the shares run from 0.08 for LLaMA 30B and 65B
\citep{touvron2023llama} to 0.89 for Qwen3 \citep{yang2025qwen3}, both read as cap or choice and so lower bounds.

\subsection{Beyond the Designs}\label{sec:wedge-pi}

Released models lie beyond the designs in two directions. Forty-six of the 77 clean models have $M$ above 341, the
largest ratio in the Chinchilla design, and 73 used more compute than $3\times10^{21}$ FLOP, the largest budget at which
any public design brackets a training optimum (Section~\ref{sec:ident-modelfree}).

\textit{Tokens per parameter.} Figure~\ref{fig:extrap} compares the local wedge of Proposition~\ref{prop:modelfree}(i),
which needs no functional form, with those of parametric forms fitted to the same runs in three recipes: Farseer's
design \citep{li2025predictableb}, Marin's IsoFLOP ladders on three corpora \citep{marin2026ladders} and Meta's Llama~3
IsoFLOP profiles \citep{grattafiori2024llama,czech2026llama3isoflop}. In all three, parametric forms understate the
wedge at high $M$, and the gap grows with $M$: at 256 to 1,024 tokens per parameter the $\kappa$-free form fitted to all
runs gives 0.65 to 0.87 of the local wedge, and the Chinchilla form fitted to runs with $M\le100$ about 0.4.

The direction replicates; the shape does not. Measured against a linear extrapolation from the local slope at the path,
the form of the $\kappa$ family (Proposition~\ref{prop:modelfree}(iii)), $\ln w$ is convex in Farseer and Marin, by 0.17
and 0.43 log points at $M$ of 256 to 1,024. In Llama~3 it is concave at the primary bandwidth, though not at every one
($-0.13$ at $M$ of 64 to 256), and a steeper local slope at the path (a local $\sigma^*$ of 0.63, against 0.69 for the
$\kappa$-free fit) makes the $\kappa$-free fit overstate the local wedge at $M$ below 16. Inside the designs, then,
parametric forms understate the wedge at high $M$, but the direction of the error beyond them is recipe-dependent. The
evidence comes from small models, and at high $M$ from few points (Online Appendix~\ref{app:wedge-pi}).

\textit{Compute.} Proposition~\ref{prop:pi} replaces a point extrapolation of the zero point with an identified set. For
each design we fit a line to the logged IsoFLOP minima at its bracketed budgets and extrapolate it with a simultaneous
95 percent band for its level and slope (a wild bootstrap-$t$): Chinchilla, anchored at $3\times10^{21}$ FLOP in total
parameters, the convention of the models' $M$; Llama~3, at $10^{21}$; and Marin, at $3\times10^{20}$, with one slope for
its three corpora, as in Section~\ref{sec:tech}. DeepSeek's published law is a fourth path. The identified set for
$M^*(10^{24})$ is then [5.7, 248]; Marin's path, the steepest, sets its upper end. The minima curve inside the designs;
continuing Chinchilla's and Llama~3's paths at their top-budget slopes, beside the bands, identifies 75 percent of
models (Online Appendix~\ref{app:wedge-pi}).

\textit{The sign.} Over-training is identified when $M$ exceeds every ratio in the identified set, whatever the curvature
and the functional form (Proposition~\ref{prop:pi}): for 78 percent of models and 69 percent of decisions. No model is
identified as under-trained, but that is mechanical: every clean model has $M\ge19$, while the lower end of the set is 4
to 11 at their compute. The 17 models whose sign is not identified all have $M$ below 250. They include Llama~2 70B,
Llama~3.1 405B, Qwen2 72B, Falcon 180B and DeepSeek LLM 67B, and they carry most of the training compute: weighted by
compute, the share identified is 24 percent over models and 14 percent over decisions. With the slopes bounded by the
designs' point estimates alone, the share is 87 percent of models.

The bands assume that a developer's recipe shares the designs' zero points, but recipes, tokenizers and data quality move
$M^*$ (Section~\ref{sec:tech:portable}); the tilt allowance $\tau$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:pi} admits such differences.
The share of models identified is 74 percent at $\tau=1.3$, for tokenizer differences; 66 percent at 1.84, DataDecide's
largest tilt under the reference exponents; and 53 percent at 3.4, the same tilt under DataDecide's own exponents.
Weighted by compute, it is 17 and 16 percent at the last two. \textcolor{red}{[TBD-m9: The experiment's tilt between its

percent of models with normal inputs alone ($|e_p|\le1$), and 23 percent with every technology's in-support zero point as

Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:app-breakdown}).

\textit{Magnitudes.} With $1/\sigma^*-1$ between 0.40 and 0.68, from the in-design estimates to the top-budget value,
the medians of the models' lower and upper bounds on $s$ are 0.41 and 0.95 (0.99 with $\sigma^*$ at the lower end of its

\subsection{Conduct and Rival Wedges}\label{sec:wedge-conduct}

The objectives that Proposition~\ref{prop:wedge} admits predict different patterns (Online Appendix
Remark~\ref{rem:A-conduct}). Serving cost predicts more over-training for sizes a developer serves and for closed
models; adoption by users who bear the cost, the most for models meant for users' devices; memory tiers, bunching at
tier boundaries. Inference uses a restricted wild cluster bootstrap by developer
\citep{cameron2008bootstrap,webb2023reworking}.

\textit{Serving.} Coded by model (this size offered through the developer's own per-token interface or consumer
product within 180 days of release), 26 of the 77 clean models were served, 19 of them by Alibaba, and 25 of the 26
share a budget with other sizes or sit in tier windows. Given compute and release year, the coefficient of $\ln\hat w$ on
the serving indicator is 0.56 (standard error 0.24), the sign that serving-cost internalization predicts, but with six
treated developers the test cannot detect a relation: the bootstrap $p$-value is 0.41. Randomization inference gives
$p<0.001$ when it permutes the code across models, treating one developer's sizes as separate assignments, but 0.10 when
it permutes it across developers \citep{mackinnon2020randomization}, and 0.94 without Alibaba. Without Alibaba the
coefficient is $-0.09$; over decisions it is 0.28 ($p$-value 0.67).

\textit{Open weights, tiers and devices.} The one test with power points away from serving cost. Among 159
production-scale decisions from 67 developers, in a broader universe than the clean sample that includes unverified
Epoch rows, open-weight decisions are more over-trained than closed ones by 0.45 log points given compute and release
year (standard error 0.17, $p$-value 0.018), whereas a developer bearing all of its serving cost should over-train at
least as much. Closed developers, however, disclosed only eight allocations in 2024 and 2025, and none for their small,
typically distilled models, so the closed sample is selected on flagships. Sizes bunch: 51 percent of the 220
open-weight production-scale models of 2023--2026 sit in the tier windows, against 13 percent under a smooth
counterfactual \citep{chetty2011adjustment,kleven2016bunching}. And the nine on-device models, from two developers, have
a median share of 0.90, against 0.70 for other targets.

The data are thus more consistent with adoption and tier mechanisms than with the internalization of serving cost, and we
read $w-1$ as the revealed value of compactness. Planned serving expenditure is fully licensed only where the developer
served the size and neither a common budget nor a tier window intervenes: Qwen2 0.5B alone (Qwen2 1.5B shares its budget
with the 7B and 72B). The cost side matters little (Online Appendix~\ref{app:wedge-cost}).

\textit{Recipes.} A developer's own recipe may place $M^*$ away from the reference, the factor-bias critique of markup
estimation \citep{demirer2020production,raval2023testing}. Where a developer published its own scaling evidence we apply
one rule: its expansion path, in its own parameter convention, with the common model-free $\sigma^*=0.687$. Llama~1
and~2, allocated before Meta's 2024 path existed, are left out. On the 15 models covered, the median share is 0.53 under
developers' own technologies, against 0.70 under the reference; AI2's ladder \citep{bhagia2024establishing}, which puts
$M^*$ at 3.7 to 6.7 times the reference zero point, drives the gap, and without AI2 the order reverses (0.71 against
0.65). Over all 49 decisions, with developers' own technologies where they exist, the median is 0.72; like the
reference's, these levels are lower bounds if $\sigma^*$ falls with compute (Online Appendix~\ref{app:wedge-labown}).

\textit{A second output.} A developer that values an output other than validation loss equates a different wedge,
$\hat w=w_{L'}\,w_L/w_{L'}$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:wedge}(iii)), the output critique of \citet{bond2020unpleasant}. On the
OLMo ladder's 30 runs, the median of $\ln(w_{\mathrm{task}}/w_{\mathrm{C4}})$ over the 77 clean models, task bits per
byte against C4 loss, is $-0.11$ with $\kappa=1$ and $-0.37$ with $\kappa$ free, neither distinguishable from zero; with
$\kappa$ free, a task-valuing developer's wedge could be a third of the loss-based one or three and a half times it. The
only second-output design available has no power, and the output critique remains unbounded.

\textit{Post-training and capability.} Post-training compute, which scales with $N$ but not with $D$, raises the wedge
to $w=1+x_P+m_N$, with $x_P$ its ratio to pretraining compute. Disclosed budgets put $x_P$ at 0.2 percent for DeepSeek-V3
\citep{deepseekai2024deepseekv3}, below 0.1 percent for the supervised stage of T\"ulu~3 \citep{lambert2024tulu3}
(converting disclosed GPU-hours at an assumed 40 percent utilization) and at most 32 percent for Olmo~3.1 Think 32B
\citep{olmo2025olmo3}. At 32 percent the median model share falls from 0.75 to 0.73, and the median decision share by
0.025. Loss, moreover, is not capability: over-trained models lose more quality under quantization
\citep{kumar2024scaling}, so $\hat w-1$ understates their value to users who run quantized models, whereas the
second-output estimates point the other way; neither direction is measured at production scale.

\subsection{The Trend since 2023}\label{sec:wedge-trends}

Under the reference technology, the compute-weighted share over decisions, $\sum_i(w_i^+-1)C_i/\sum_iw_i^+C_i$ with
$w_i^+=\max(w_i,1)$, rose from 0.24 among 2023 releases to 0.54 in 2024 and 0.81 in 2025, and over models from 0.27 to
0.83. A bootstrap over the 18 developers establishes the rise from 2023 to 2025, with a 95 percent interval for the
change of [0.19, 0.72], and from 2023 to 2024. It does not establish the step from 2024 to 2025 ([$-0.12$, 0.44]). The
weighted shares rise in both steps under all 32 technologies and in each of the 18 samples that leave out one developer,
but the median decision share rose from 0.49 to 0.77 and then fell to 0.74. The level does not travel: the 2025 weighted
share over decisions ranges from 0.23 to 0.92 across technologies (Online Appendix~\ref{app:wedge-trends}).

Five qualifications apply. First, it largely restates the rise in tokens per parameter, whose median rose from 146 in
2023 to 1,217 in 2024 and 2,438 in 2025. Second, part of the weighted rise is composition: Llama~3.1 405B, whose sign is
not identified, carries 55 percent of 2024 compute, and the sign is least identified in the base year, 2023 (12 percent
of its compute). Third, 2025 is thin, with 13 dense models in six decisions; without any one of Alibaba, Hugging Face or
Marin the median decision share falls further. Fourth, at $x_P$ of 32 percent the 2025 weighted share over models falls
from 0.83 to 0.82, or to 0.77 with post-training compute in the cost base. Fifth, 92 percent of 2025 clean-sample
compute sits in tier windows, so the rise may reflect tiers' shadow values, and the excluded mixture-of-experts models
carried more 2025 open-weight compute than the clean sample; with them the 2025 share is 0.73 at total and 0.82 at
active parameters. With developer fixed effects, identified by the nine developers with decisions at more than one date, the
wedge grew 1.6-fold a year (95 percent interval [0.91, 2.61]). Most technologies, the reference among them, date from 2022 to 2024, and better data
shift the optimum toward parameters \citep{bi2024deepseek}, so improving data would make them understate the rise, and
parameter-augmenting architectural change overstate it.

The rise is a rise in revealed over-training; it is a rise in the value of compactness only if developers optimized
against technologies like ours. Among decisions whose developers state a deployment rationale the median share rose from
0.48 in 2023 to 0.89 in 2025, while the share of decisions citing a compute-optimal target fell from 0.12 to zero (Online
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:app-rationale}).

We do not extend the series before 2023. OPT-175B and GLM-130B, with 92 percent of the compute of the eight open-weight
production-scale releases of 2019--2022, were allocated when the law of \citet{kaplan2020scaling} guided practice; with
Kaplan's path as the believed technology (Proposition~\ref{prop:wedge}(iii)), the period's compute-weighted share is 0.19
to 0.29 rather than 0.04 (0.43 to 0.57 with Kaplan's curvature as well).

Public data cannot validate levels (Online Appendices~\ref{app:wedge-conduct} and~\ref{app:wedge-trends}). The level, a
marginal value of compactness of about 2.8 percent of training cost per percent of size for the median decision, remains
conditional on a zero point that no public design observes at the relevant compute.
