\section{The Technology: Additional Results}\label{app:additional}
\setlength{\emergencystretch}{2em}% allow looser lines in the online appendix

This appendix supports Sections~\ref{sec:framework} through~\ref{sec:tech}. Appendix~\ref{app:add-dictionary} gives
the full dictionary between scaling-law practice and production economics. Appendix~\ref{app:add-chinchilla} reports
estimators, inference, exclusion rules and duality tests on the Chinchilla data, and the same comparisons on the IsoFLOP
designs that labs have published. Appendix~\ref{app:add-modelfree} documents the finite-grid properties and the
specification sensitivity of the model-free estimator of Proposition~\ref{prop:modelfree} and its application to
Farseer's grid. Appendix~\ref{app:add-tech} collects robustness results for
the parametric technologies across sweeps, Appendix~\ref{app:add-overhead} translates the elasticity of substitution
into the compute cost of over-training, and Appendix~\ref{app:add-measurement} treats input measurement and flexible
inputs.

\subsection{A Dictionary}\label{app:add-dictionary}

Table~\ref{tab:app-dictionary} lists nineteen correspondences between scaling-law concepts and concepts of production
theory and IO econometrics. The entries in panel~A are identities of duality theory once training compute is measured
by the FLOP-accounting approximation $C\approx6ND$. The others are analogies: the econometric problem is the same, but
its source can differ.

Three analogies break down. First, parameters and tokens have no market prices. FLOP accounting gives every lab the
same log-cost weights, which is why optimizing labs share one expansion path, but a FLOP is not a dollar, and
size-dependent prices of training and serving FLOPs enter the first-order conditions as wedges
(Proposition~\ref{prop:wedge}). Second, returns to scale measured in loss depend on how output is cardinalized, so
``diminishing returns to compute'' has no unit-free content; the elasticity of substitution, the optimal mix and the
wedge do. Third, the inputs of a designed sweep are assigned, not chosen. An IsoFLOP sweep is designed variation in the
input mix, not a shock to relative prices, and it elicits no behavioral response; the endogeneity machinery of IO
applies to cross-lab data (Online Appendix~\ref{app:obs}), not to single-lab experiments.

Two correspondences are narrower than their names suggest. The collinearity of inputs along the expansion path is the
problem of optimizing firms that face common prices \citep{marschak1944random}, in the sharp form of
\citet{bond2005adjustment}: identification requires relative-price variation, adjustment frictions or optimization
errors. It is not the functional dependence of \citet{ackerberg2015identification}, which concerns the first stage of
proxy estimators and arises here only in cross-lab data (Online Appendix~\ref{app:proofs},
Proposition~\ref{prop:proxy}). And the wedge $w=\varepsilon_N/\varepsilon_D$ is the ratio statistic of
\citet{raval2023testing} with FLOP-cost weights in place of revenue shares. Raval reads a ratio different from one as
misspecification; Proposition~\ref{prop:wedge} reads it as a value of compactness. Cross-lab factor bias is therefore
the first threat to the inversion (Online Appendix~\ref{app:wedge}).

\subsection{The Chinchilla Data}\label{app:add-chinchilla}

\emph{Design.}---Epoch AI's digitization of Figure~4 of \citet{hoffmann2022training} contains 245 runs with training
compute from $1.4\times10^{18}$ to $1.3\times10^{22}$ FLOP \citep{besiroglu2024chinchilla}. Only 137 of them lie on
the nine IsoFLOP profiles, whose budgets run from $6\times10^{18}$ to $3\times10^{21}$ FLOP. The other 108 are
off-profile runs that form 35 streaks of fixed model size: the training-horizon runs that Hoffmann et al.\ pool into
their Approach~3. The baseline sample drops the five highest-loss runs ($n=240$, with 132 profile runs). In it, 33
percent of the variance of centered $(\ln N,\ln D)$ lies across the expansion path: 37 percent among the profile runs
and 21 percent among the off-profile runs. The IsoFLOP-minima objects below use the 132 profile runs; the primal uses
all 240.

\emph{Estimators.}---Table~\ref{tab:app-estimators} fits the Chinchilla form with five estimators. Huber loss with
$\delta=10^{-3}$ on log loss, the estimator of \citet{besiroglu2024chinchilla}, is least absolute deviations in practice:
84 percent of its residuals lie in the linear region of the loss, and the two agree to the third decimal. Least squares,
on log loss or in levels, weights the few large residuals more heavily and gives a larger data exponent; the paired
differences in $\beta$, 0.039 and 0.060, are two to three bootstrap standard errors. The implied compute-optimal ratios
at $5.76\times10^{23}$ FLOP are lower by 3.7 and 7.6 tokens per parameter, less than one and a half standard errors.
Estimating the loss equation jointly with the nine IsoFLOP argmins under the cross-equation restrictions of
Proposition~\ref{prop:duality} leaves the estimates unchanged; the restrictions are not rejected ($p=0.60$). Panel~B
restates Proposition~\ref{prop:ident} on these data. The 41 runs within 0.15 log points of the expansion path carry less
than 1 percent of their input variance across the path, and the condition number of the Jacobian rises from 62 on the
full design to 413 on them, against 69 to 95 for random subsamples of the same size. In raw parameters the condition
number of the full design is about 2,000, which is why the standard errors that \citet{besiroglu2024chinchilla} report
for $A$ and $B$ (125 and 1,293) are large: they measure the levels at $N=D=1$, far outside the design, and are not
evidence that the design fails to identify the technology.

\emph{Inference.}---Table~\ref{tab:app-chin-inference} compares six bootstrap schemes for the Huber refit. Because it
behaves as a median-type estimator, the wild bootstraps use the weights of \citet{feng2011wild}. The restriction
$\kappa=1$ is rejected at 1 percent under every scheme except nine budget clusters that assign each off-profile run
to the nearest budget ($p=0.052$). That assignment is not a model of the
dependence in the data, because an off-profile run from a $10^{22}$-FLOP trunk is not part of the $3\times10^{21}$
IsoFLOP experiment. Clustering the off-profile runs by their model-size trunk lowers the standard error of $\beta$ from
0.050 to 0.042 and moves $p(\kappa=1)$ to 0.004. It also narrows the 95 percent interval for $M^*$ at
$5.76\times10^{23}$ FLOP from [3.7, 179] to [5.2, 106]. Treating the off-profile runs as singleton clusters gives nearly
the same answers. Our preferred scheme is the design-conditional wild bootstrap; the budget-and-trunk clusters are the conservative
check.

The CES restriction $\alpha=\beta$ is not rejected by any test that is robust to the heavy tails of the residuals, whose
MAD-based standard deviation is 0.0049 against a standard deviation of 0.0076. The Gaussian likelihood ratio of 10.4 has
a $\chi^2_1$ $p$-value of 0.001, but its restricted-bootstrap 95 percent critical value is 20.2, not 3.84, which gives
$p=0.17$. Heteroskedasticity-robust Wald tests give 0.060 and 0.089, so the evidence against CES is borderline at 10
percent and absent at 5. The Chinchilla-70B wedge contains one under every scheme, and it excludes the values of 0.62
to 0.71 that Hoffmann et al.'s published parameters imply only under the pairs and wild schemes. Because DeepMind sized
that model with Approaches 1 and 2 on these same runs, $w(70\text{B})\approx1$ is an internal-consistency check with
little power, not a test of revealed preference.

\emph{Profiles.}---Figure~\ref{fig:app-kappa-profile} plots the profile likelihood over $\sigma^*$ with $\kappa$ free,
relative to the unconstrained optimum. On the full design, the Huber (Laplace quasi-likelihood) profile has its minimum
at $\hat\sigma^*=0.701$ and a $\chi^2$-based 95 percent set of [0.69, 0.71]. A restricted wild bootstrap of the
statistic widens the set to [0.68, 0.72] and rejects the value that $\kappa=1$ implies, 0.74 (likelihood ratio 29.0,
$p=0.003$, the smallest value the bootstrap can deliver). The Gaussian profile has its minimum at 0.680 and a set of
[0.67, 0.69], which belongs with the Gaussian point estimate rather than the Huber one. On the 41 runs within 0.15 log points of the expansion path,
the profile is flat: the quasi-likelihood ratio stays below 1.21 between 0.25 and 0.94, and the 95 percent set reaches
the upper edge of the grid. On-path runs carry no information about $\sigma^*$, as Proposition~\ref{prop:ident}
implies.

\emph{Exclusion rules.}---Table~\ref{tab:app-selection} shows how the estimates move as the $k$ highest-loss runs are
dropped. The data exponent $\beta$ falls almost monotonically, from 0.453 with all 245 runs to 0.347 at $k=15$, while
$\alpha$ stays between 0.345 and 0.349. The five runs that \citet{besiroglu2024chinchilla} drop are the most
data-starved runs of the $10^{19}$ budget ($D/N$ between 0.04 and 0.40), with log residuals 9 to 45 times the residual
standard deviation. Their influence is leverage in the $D/N\to0$ corner of the design, not truncation bias: a
truncated-Gaussian likelihood that conditions on the exclusion rule \citep{hausman1977social} returns the estimate that
ignores truncation ($\beta=0.406$ in both), and dropping runs by their data-to-parameter ratio rather than their loss
gives the same answer as dropping them by loss. The Chinchilla-70B wedge is 1.54 with the outliers. Across twelve rules
that remove them it lies between 0.95 and 1.06 for ten; dropping the whole $10^{19}$ budget gives 0.78, and the
IsoFLOP-profile runs alone give 1.16.

\emph{Duality.}---Table~\ref{tab:app-duality} reports the objects that each estimation approach delivers and tests the
cross-equation restrictions of Proposition~\ref{prop:duality}. We call the curve fitted through the nine IsoFLOP minima
the IsoFLOP-minima frontier. It is not Hoffmann et al.'s Approach~1, whose training-curve envelopes the digitization
does not contain and which reports allocation exponents as well as a frontier. Tests must be design-consistent.
Comparing the IsoFLOP argmins with the analytic primal path rejects even our own refit under the stratified bootstrap,
because parabolas fitted on a finite grid are biased \citep{czech2026problems}. Against design-consistent nulls, our
refit and Besiroglu et al.'s parameters are consistent with the argmins ($p\ge0.16$ in every bootstrap-calibrated and
classical variant). Hoffmann et al.'s published Approach~3 is marginally inconsistent (classical $p=0.040$; calibrated
$p=0.060$), mainly because its compute-optimal model size at the center of the design is about 12 percent below the
argmins. The frontier restrictions are rejected under the stratified bootstrap but not under the wild bootstrap. The
IsoFLOP-minima frontier lies 0.005 to 0.008 nats below every primal frontier, a gap of the order of the quantization of the
digitized losses, which are read from a color map, and we do not interpret it.

\emph{Lab-own designs.}---Table~\ref{tab:app-labs} applies the same comparison to the IsoFLOP experiments that labs have
published. Our argmin fit reproduces Meta's Llama~3 law exactly. The primal fitted to the same runs implies a steeper
path ($a=0.527$ against 0.463), in which $M^*$ falls rather than rises with compute; the difference is significant at 5
percent once the primal's own sampling error is included ($t=-2.11$) and not under the wild bootstrap. Two of Meta's ten
budgets do not bracket their minimum. Fitted on the other eight, the path slope is 0.50 and $M^*(10^{24})$ is 15 instead
of 31 (Online Appendix Table~\ref{tab:app-labown}). For the Marin ladders the design-consistent tests do not reject
($p$ between 0.33 and 0.54). About three-quarters of the naive discrepancy is finite-grid parabola bias and the rest is
FLOP accounting: $6ND$ differs from Marin's nominal budgets by $-7$ to $+35$ percent. The Marin primal exponents
($\alpha$ between 0.64 and 0.69) are far from Chinchilla's, a first indication that exponents are specific to the
recipe.

\subsection{The Model-Free Estimator}\label{app:add-modelfree}

\emph{Finite grids.}---A least-squares quadratic fitted to an IsoFLOP profile on a finite grid overstates the curvature
at the minimum, because the profile's quartic term loads on the quadratic coefficient. To leading order the bias is
$(f^{(4)}/24)\kappa_4(h)$, where $\kappa_4$ depends only on the moments of the grid (Online Appendix~\ref{app:proofs},
remark after Proposition~\ref{prop:A-modelfree}). Table~\ref{tab:app-finite-grid} evaluates it for nine runs centered at
the minimum of the Chinchilla profile at $10^{21}$ FLOP. A grid spanning a factor of 9 in $N$ overstates the curvature by
1.3 percent and gives $\hat\sigma^*=0.7345$ against a true 0.7370. A grid spanning a factor of 100 overstates it by 5.9
percent ($\hat\sigma^*=0.7257$). A quartic removes the bias in both cases, at the cost of variance. Within a span of
about 3 in $N$, the bias of a quadratic is below 0.3 percent of the curvature. The argmin is biased too; this is the
finite-grid bias of IsoFLOP argmins documented by \citet{czech2026problems}, and it is why the duality tests of
Appendix~\ref{app:add-chinchilla} use design-consistent nulls.

\emph{Specification.}---The primary estimator of Section~\ref{sec:tech} fits a quadratic in $\ln N$ to the runs within
one log point of a pooled Approach-2 path, on budgets whose minimum is bracketed by at least two runs on each side, and
pools the budgets by a random-effects mean. Table~\ref{tab:app-mf-sens} reports its sensitivity. Pooled $F$ tests of a
cubic term never reject at 5 percent. Across window widths, polynomial orders, frontier smoothers and window centering,
the random-effects $\sigma^*$ ranges over 0.665--0.714 for Chinchilla (0.603 with the narrowest window, which keeps seven
budgets), 0.642--0.677 for Llama~3 and 0.64--0.72 for Marin. An independent re-implementation that shares no code gives
0.669--0.694 on the same five designs. In a Monte Carlo at each design's own fitted $\kappa$-family technology, the
random-effects interval covers the truth in 92 to 98 percent of replications, with a bias of at most 0.005. The pooled
design-conditional interval under-covers (82 to 91 percent), which is why the random-effects summary is primary.
Without noise, the pooled bias is at most 0.006 in absolute value on every design except the strongly curved
$\kappa$-family fit to Porian et al.'s profiles ($+0.047$ to $+0.050$).

The estimator needs exact isocosts, so each design is analyzed in the parameter convention in which its budgets equal
$6ND$. The convention matters where FLOP accounting varies with size. On Marin, whose accounting is observed, the
configuration count gives $\sigma^*$ of 0.64 to 0.66, 0.04 to 0.07 below the FLOP-implied count. If Chinchilla's
FLOPs per token varied with $N$ with elasticity $\eta_C$, the measured curvature would rescale by $(1+\eta_C)^{-2}$ and
$\sigma^*$ would move by about $0.44\eta_C$, at most 0.04 for $|\eta_C|\le0.1$. Rebuilding $D$ from Hoffmann et al.'s
architecture table would remove this uncertainty; we have not done so.

\emph{Farseer's local expansion path.}---Farseer's grid has no IsoFLOP profiles, so we estimate the loss surface
locally. A Gaussian product-kernel local quadratic of $\ln L$ in $(\ln N,\ln D)$, with bandwidths of 0.24 and 0.74 in
logs from leave-one-out cross-validation, gives the two output elasticities at every point and hence the local wedge
$w=(L_c-L_x)/(L_c+L_x)$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:modelfree}(i), which is invariant to monotone transformations of the
loss. On ten isocosts from $10^{18}$ to $10^{21}$ FLOP we locate the point inside the design where $w=1$; it exists on
eight, from $5\times10^{18}$ FLOP up, with compute-optimal ratios of 25 to 45. Two estimators of $\sigma^*$ follow. The
first evaluates the elasticity of substitution from the local gradient and Hessian at these points: a random-effects
mean of 0.703 (standard error 0.013) that falls by 0.015 per decade of compute (0.008). Second derivatives need more
smoothing than the surface itself, and this estimate moves between 0.66 and 0.73 with the bandwidth; at the narrower
cross-validated optimum on an extended grid it is 0.664. The second estimator uses only first derivatives. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:modelfree}(iii), the slope of $\ln w$ in $u=\ln(M/M^*(C))$ at $u=0$ equals $1/\sigma^*-1$ for any
smooth technology, so a regression of the local $\ln w$ on $u$ and $u^2$ gives $\sigma^*=1/(1+b_1)$ from the linear
coefficient $b_1$: 0.708 (wild cluster standard error by model size 0.001), between 0.701 and 0.711 under every bandwidth,
and 0.670 with embedding parameters counted. Section~\ref{sec:tech} reports this estimate; its standard error treats the
grid points as the design and excludes smoothing bias. The quadratic coefficient, 0.020 (0.001), shows that $\ln w$ is
convex in $u$, which no member of the $\kappa$ family allows, since each makes $\ln w$ linear in $u$; Online
Appendix~\ref{app:wedge-pi} uses the convexity to sign the error of parametric wedges at high $M$.

\emph{Drift with compute.}---Within Chinchilla and Llama~3 the per-budget estimates fall with compute, by 0.072 (s.e.\
0.026) and 0.053 (0.010) per decade. Marin shows no drift over its two decades, and Porian et al.'s unannealed profiles
drift the other way. Neither the Chinchilla form nor the $\kappa$ family lets $\sigma^*$ vary along the path, and under
those constant-$\sigma^*$ technologies fitted to Chinchilla and Llama~3 the estimator's noise-free per-budget bias lies
within $[-0.005,+0.002]$, so the drift is not a finite-grid artifact. It is, however, window-sensitive in Chinchilla
($-0.034$ to $-0.096$ per decade), and no design spans more than 2.7 decades of compute.

\emph{Porian et al.'s profiles.}---These give $\sigma^*$ near 0.51, well below every other design, and are
window-sensitive: 0.45 to 0.55 across windows and 0.33 with a global quadratic. They are read off constant-learning-rate
runs without cooldown. The gap between such a loss and an annealed loss plausibly varies with $D/N$ at fixed compute,
which bends the profile. We therefore treat them as IsoFLOP-like rather than IsoFLOP designs and keep them out of the
range reported in Section~\ref{sec:tech}; with them, the random-effects mean across designs falls from 0.695 to 0.646 and
heterogeneity becomes large ($Q=189$ on 7 degrees of freedom).

\subsection{The Technology across Sweeps}\label{app:add-tech}

\emph{Specification tests.}---Table~\ref{tab:app-spec-tests} reports the tests for the seven sweep--corpus technologies
from five studies. The restriction $\kappa=1$ is rejected in every sweep. CES is not rejected by the Huber-based Wald
test in any sweep ($p$ between 0.22 and 0.90). The Gaussian likelihood ratio rejects it on Chinchilla and is borderline
on Farseer ($p=0.052$) and the OLMo ladder (0.067), but on Chinchilla the rejection does not survive calibration for
heavy tails (Appendix~\ref{app:add-chinchilla}). Rank-one curvature, which the $\kappa$ family imposes on a translog in
$\ln(L-E)$, is rejected in two sweeps when $E$ comes from the Chinchilla form and in one (Farseer) when it comes from
the $\kappa$ family.

\emph{The outer exponent free.}---Table~\ref{tab:app-kappa-robust} reports $\sigma^*_\kappa$ with design-conditional
wild standard errors and under changes of sample, convention and irreducible loss. On Farseer it is stable across
size exclusions and samples (0.694 to 0.716) except for the parameter-count convention: counting both embedding matrices
lowers it to 0.668. On Chinchilla it falls to 0.690 and 0.648 when the smallest tenth and quarter of the runs are
dropped, and to 0.664 when the five outliers are kept. The small sweeps are fragile. On Gadre et al.'s corpora
$\sigma^*_\kappa$ moves between 0.48 and 0.70 when one or two of the six sizes are dropped, leaving 18 to 27 runs. The
trade-off between $E$ and $\kappa$ is immaterial inside the confidence sets for $E$ on Chinchilla, Farseer and the OLMo
ladder (at most 0.006) but material on Gadre et al.'s RedPajama (0.606 to 0.685) and on Muennighoff et al.\ (0.503 to
0.555). Only one variant moves a small sweep near 0.70: RedPajama without its two smallest sizes, from 19 runs with a
standard error of 0.097. The heterogeneity of $\sigma^*_\kappa$ across sweeps in Section~\ref{sec:tech} may therefore
partly reflect how the $\kappa$ family fits small designs without IsoFLOP profiles, rather than technology.

\emph{The Chinchilla form.}---Table~\ref{tab:app-robust} reports the $\kappa=1$ estimates under alternative
conventions, outputs and samples. Conventions matter most for the allocation exponent. Counting Farseer's two untied
embedding matrices moves $a$ from 0.526 to 0.411 and $\sigma^*$ from 0.772 to 0.724. Dropping the two single-epoch runs
in which \citet{muennighoff2023scaling} trained 1.1 and 2.8 billion parameters on 100 million tokens moves $a$ from 0.535
to 0.360 and $M^*(10^{21})$ from 60 to 139. With task bits per byte instead of C4 cross-entropy, the OLMo ladder's
$\sigma^*$ falls from 0.825 to 0.763 (and $\sigma^*_\kappa$ from 0.544 to 0.512). Ordinality guarantees invariance only
to transformations of the same output; two outputs can have different isoquants, so such differences can reflect
technology as well as sampling noise and misspecification.

\emph{Neutrality of data quality.}---Table~\ref{tab:app-neutrality} nests hypotheses about how better data enter. What
matters for allocation and for the wedge is the tilt $\ln(A_r/B_r)$, which is ordinal; asymptote shifts and neutral
scalings of reducible loss leave the isoquant map unchanged. Across the three web corpora of \citet{gadre2024language},
a Hicks-neutral scaling of reducible loss with corpus-specific asymptotes is not rejected ($p=0.90$) and explains 96.8
percent of the cross-corpus variation. The test has little power: the data- and parameter-augmenting alternatives are
not rejected either ($p=0.13$ and 0.15). Across the 25 DataDecide recipes \citep{magnusson2025datadecide} every
restriction is rejected, at the resolution of the bootstrap (0.01 with 99 draws, 0.05 with 19 draws). Asymptote shifts
alone explain 98.1 percent of the variation, and a neutral scale brings this to 99.7 percent. The remaining tilt is
small in fit but not in allocation: it spans 0.22 to 0.26 across recipes, implies compute-optimal ratios that differ by a
factor of 2.9 to 3.4, and moves revealed wedges by a factor of 1.25 to 1.29 at the same inputs. Because DataDecide's
checkpoints come before the learning-rate schedule ends, the comparison assumes that the unfinished schedule affects all
recipes alike. \citet{bi2024deepseek} report the related finding that higher-quality data shift the compute-optimal
allocation toward model size.

\emph{Functional form on Farseer.}---Table~\ref{tab:app-farseer} compares the Chinchilla form with richer forms on the
Farseer grid \citep{li2025predictableb}. The Chinchilla form extrapolates poorly in compute: its out-of-sample error on
the top decile of compute is 8 to 10 times that of Farseer's nine-parameter form. On the local expansion path, the
model-free estimate of $\sigma^*$ is 0.708, against 0.710 with $\kappa$ free and 0.772 in the Chinchilla form.

\subsection{The Compute Cost of Over-Training}\label{app:add-overhead}

Practitioners use the elasticity of substitution through one quantity: the extra training compute needed to reach the
loss of the compute-optimal model while training at $k$ times the compute-optimal ratio $M^*$.
Table~\ref{tab:app-overhead} reports it. Over-training by a factor of 10 costs 39 percent ($\sigma^*=0.8$) to 124 percent
($\sigma^*=0.6$) more compute; by a factor of 100 it costs 3.5 to 14 times the minimum, and by 1,000 it costs 14 to 129
times. The curvature matters more as $k$ grows. At $k=100$, $\sigma^*=0.70$ implies 7.2 times the minimum compute, and
$\sigma^*=0.74$, the value that the Chinchilla form imposes on the Chinchilla data, implies 5.4 times. The entries
assume a homothetic split of the exponents; the splits of \citet{besiroglu2024chinchilla} and
\citet{hoffmann2022training} change them by at most 4.5 and 19 percent for $k\le100$.

Figure~\ref{fig:app-overhead} compares these curves with the compute-overhead curve of \citet{devries2023go}, which lies
between our curves for $\sigma^*=0.74$ and 0.80; with his parameters, a model at 30 percent of the compute-optimal size
needs 99 percent more compute, as our formula implies. The implied wedge $w=k^{1/\sigma^*-1}$ links the table to
Section~\ref{sec:wedge}. A developer who trains 100 times beyond $M^*$ reveals an expenditure share of the value of
compactness, $s=(w-1)/w$, of 0.68 to 0.95, depending on $\sigma^*$.

\subsection{Measurement and Flexible Inputs}\label{app:add-measurement}

The largest disagreement in the scaling literature concerns the allocation exponent: $N^*\propto C^{0.73}$ in
\citet{kaplan2020scaling} against about 0.5 in \citet{hoffmann2022training}. \citet{porian2024resolving} trace it to a
sequence of changes in training practice, and \citet{pearce2024reconciling} to the treatment of embedding parameters.
Both are classic problems in production-function estimation. Counting the parameters that cost FLOPs, such as the
output head, is input measurement, whose bias depends on how the measurement error correlates with scale
\citep{collardwexler2016production}. Warmup, learning-rate schedule, learning rate and batch size are flexible inputs;
when they are not re-optimized at each scale, they are not concentrated out of the production function
\citep{ackerberg2015identification,gandhi2020identification}.

\emph{Decomposition.}---Re-estimating Porian et al.'s path from their 975 released runs reproduces all ten published
exponents to within 0.003 (Table~\ref{tab:app-measurement}, panel~A). The exponent falls from 0.837 to 0.496 on
RefinedWeb and from 0.862 to 0.517 on OpenWebText2. Counting the head accounts for 39 and 47 percent of the drop, and
flexible inputs for the remaining 53 to 61 percent. Because a sequential decomposition depends on the order of the
steps, panel~B holds the runs fixed and changes only the count. Counting the head lowers $a$ by 0.12 to 0.17 whether or
not the runs are tuned, and tuning lowers it by 0.19 to 0.23 under every count, so the two components are nearly
additive. The switch from training to validation loss between steps 1 and 2 accounts for at most 0.005 of the
measurement step. These models have 5M to 901M parameters, and the shares need not hold at larger scale.

\emph{Input measurement.}\label{app:add-biasformulas}---Let the true technology be $L=E+AN^{-\alpha}+BD^{-\beta}$, and
let the researcher count $N_m$ parameters when the true count is $N=N_m+X(N_m)$. The omitted component has share
$q\equiv X/N$ and a constant scale elasticity $\theta_X\equiv d\ln X/d\ln N_m<1$. The researcher measures compute as
$C_m=6N_mD$ and takes the IsoFLOP argmin in measured units: $n_m\equiv\ln N_m$ minimizes $L$ subject to
$\ln D=c_m-\ln6-n_m$, with $c_m\equiv\ln C_m$. Because $d\ln N/dn_m=1-q(1-\theta_X)\equiv\vartheta$, the first-order
condition is $\alpha u\vartheta=\beta v$, with $u=AN^{-\alpha}$ and $v=BD^{-\beta}$. Differentiating its log along the
researcher's path, with $d\ln u/dn_m=-\alpha\vartheta$, $d\ln v=\beta\,(dn_m-dc_m)$ and $\zeta\equiv d\ln\vartheta/dn_m$,
gives $(\zeta-\alpha\vartheta)\,dn_m/dc_m=\beta\,dn_m/dc_m-\beta$, so the local measured allocation exponent is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:app-measbias}
a_m\equiv\frac{dn_m}{dc_m}=\frac{\beta}{\beta+\alpha\vartheta-\zeta},\quad
\vartheta\equiv1-q(1-\theta_X),\quad \zeta=\frac{(1-\theta_X)\,q\,(\vartheta-\theta_X)}{\vartheta}\ge0 .
\end{equation}
Since $\vartheta\le1$ and $\zeta\ge0$, $a_m\ge a=\beta/(\alpha+\beta)$, with equality when $q=0$ or $\theta_X=1$. A
proportional error ($\theta_X=1$) moves only the level of the path. The bias comes from the part of the error that is
correlated with scale, as in \citet{collardwexler2016production}, and it vanishes at scale as the omitted share falls:
a local elasticity estimated over a restricted size range is not the structural one, which is the functional-form lesson
of \citet{nerlove1963returns}. As $q\to1$, $a_m\to\beta/(\beta+\alpha\theta_X)$, the small-scale limit of
\citet{pearce2024reconciling}. The closed form matches brute-force argmins to within $2\times10^{-7}$ in $\ln N$.

With Pearce and Song's embedding map ($\theta_X=1/3$), equation~\eqref{eq:app-measbias} predicts a measured exponent over
Kaplan et al.'s size range of 0.792 under Besiroglu et al.'s parameters and 0.757 under Hoffmann et al.'s, against the
0.78 and 0.74 that Pearce and Song obtain by simulation. The small-scale limit is sensitive to how the omitted component
scales: under Besiroglu et al.'s parameters it is 0.76 at $\theta_X=1/3$ and 0.68 at $\theta_X=1/2$. On Porian et al.'s
ladder, where the omitted component is the head (its share falls from 0.94 at 5M parameters to 0.08 at 901M, with a
fitted $\theta_X$ of 0.36), the formula gets the sign and order of magnitude of the measurement bias right but
over-predicts it, by 20 to 50 percent in a semi-synthetic check on the actual design. The Chinchilla data are not
immune: with non-embedding counts the allocation exponent rises from 0.514 to 0.556 (paired difference 0.042, s.e.\
0.006) and $\sigma^*$ from 0.737 to 0.753.

\emph{Flexible inputs.}\label{app:add-flexible}---Let observed loss be $L_{\rm obs}=L^*e^{\iota(N,D)}$, where $L^*$ is
the technology with the hyperparameters concentrated out and $\iota\ge0$ is the inefficiency of the researcher's
hyperparameter policy. Along the IsoFLOP curve at compute $C$, write $f(n)$ for $L^*$ at $d=c-\ln6-n$, with minimizer
$n^*(c)$. Then $f'=-\alpha u+\beta v$ and $f''=\alpha^2u+\beta^2v$, and at the minimizer $\alpha u=\beta v=\gamma R^*$,
with $R^*$ the reducible loss on the frontier, so $f''=(\alpha+\beta)\gamma R^*$. The observed argmin solves
$f'(n)+\Delta(n)=0$, where $\Delta\equiv\partial(L_{\rm obs}-L^*)/\partial n$ at fixed compute. To first order in
$\Delta$, the log argmin shifts by $-\Delta(C)/f''(C)$, and differentiating in $\ln C$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:app-flexbias}
a_{\rm obs}-a=-\frac{\partial\,[\Delta/f'']}{\partial\ln C}.
\end{equation}
At the minimizer $\Delta=L^*e^{\iota}(\iota_n-\iota_d)$, with $\iota_n\equiv\partial\iota/\partial\ln N$ and
$\iota_d\equiv\partial\iota/\partial\ln D$, so inefficiency that depends on the inputs only through compute
($\iota_n=\iota_d$) leaves $a$ unbiased. With small, constant log-gradients, $L^*=E+R^*$ and $R^*\propto C^{-\gamma}$
give $a_{\rm obs}-a=-(\iota_n-\iota_d)E/[(\alpha+\beta)R^*(C)]$. The bias in $a$ is first order in the gradient,
although by the envelope theorem the effect on the loss level is second order in the hyperparameter error. Under the
\citet{besiroglu2024chinchilla} technology, equation~\eqref{eq:app-flexbias} matches simulated IsoFLOP argmins to within
0.003 in $a$.

On Porian et al.'s runs the formula explains about half to all (47 to 114 percent, depending on how $f''$ is estimated)
of the three flexible-input steps. The mechanism is Kaplan et al.'s fixed warmup of 1.57 billion tokens and large
batch, which leave short runs with too few useful steps. At $2.5\times10^{16}$ FLOP the untuned runs' excess loss at the
tuned optimum is 1.57 nats (the coincidence with the warmup length is numerical), and it vanishes by about
$3\times10^{18}$ FLOP. A penalty of this kind pushes small-budget argmins toward smaller models, by 0.7 to 1.2 log
points, and steepens the fitted path (Figure~\ref{fig:app-measurement}, panel b).

\emph{Hyperparameters on the Step Law grid.}---The Step Law grid of \citet{li2025predictablea}, 1,911 runs in 17
$(N,D)$ cells with up to 120 learning-rate and batch-size configurations per cell, shows the flexible inputs directly.
Table~\ref{tab:app-steplaw} shows that this design does not identify the allocation exponent: the sum of squared
residuals varies by less than 17 percent for $E$ between 0.6 and 1.7, over which $a$ ranges from 0.76 to 0.45. Holding
$E$ fixed, published hyperparameter rules shift $a$ by $-0.041$ to $+0.076$, all within 0.7 residual-bootstrap standard
errors. Choosing one random configuration per cell costs mainly precision for $a$ (median bias $-0.3$ standard errors)
but biases $\sigma^*$ by $-0.051$, or $-3.7$ standard errors. These data therefore give no reason to regard $\sigma^*$ as
the more robust object: relative to its own sampling error it moves more than $a$ along the profile in $E$ (7.0 against
2.8 standard errors).

Inefficiency falls with training tokens but not detectably with parameters (Table~\ref{tab:app-hyper}, panel~A): the scale of
inefficiency has an elasticity of $-0.23$ to $-0.26$ with respect to $D$ and an insignificant one with respect to $N$, so
longer horizons flatten the loss surface in the hyperparameters. Because the grid is designed, its minimum measures the
frontier directly, and the stochastic frontier adds only this variance function. By equation~\eqref{eq:app-flexbias},
the implied bias for a researcher who uses random configurations is small ($-0.012$ in $a$). Panel~B of the same table
estimates the demand for the flexible inputs themselves. The unconditional learning-rate demand is close to Step Law's
published rule. Holding the batch fixed, the positive token elasticity of the optimal learning rate shrinks to between
0.02 and 0.05, the distinction between conditional and unconditional demand of \citet{milgrom1996lechatelier}. This
removes the positive sign of Step Law's elasticity but not the gap to the negative elasticity of \citet{bjorck2024scaling}:
conditioning closes 26 to 43 percent of the gap to their joint estimate of $-0.32$, and less against their steeper
per-size estimates for small models.

The allocation exponent is thus the object most exposed to measurement conventions and to flexible inputs that are not
re-optimized at each scale. Section~\ref{sec:concl} draws the implications for how scaling studies should report it.
