Training a frontier language model has become one of the largest single investments in the technology sector. The
compute used to train frontier models has grown about fivefold a year since 2018 (Section~\ref{sec:econ}), in line
with \citet{sevilla2024training}, and the amortized cost of the largest training runs about 2.4-fold a year since 2016
\citep{cottier2024rising}. Developers plan these runs with neural scaling laws, empirical relations between a model's
loss $L$ on held-out text, its number of parameters $N$ and the number of tokens $D$ it is trained on
\citep{kaplan2020scaling, hoffmann2022training}. Training costs about $6ND$ floating-point operations (FLOP), and a
deployed model costs about $2N$ FLOP for every token it processes. A scaling law is therefore a production function
with a known cost side, and the choice of $N$ and $D$ is a cost-minimization problem whose answer depends on expected
use: a developer that expects heavy use trains a smaller model on more data \citep{sardana2024beyond}. Two objects
govern this choice: how easily parameters and data substitute, and how much developers value a compact model. Both
enter forecasts of data demand, of the energy and capital that serving will absorb, and of the returns to compute in
models of AI and growth.

This paper asks what developers' data reveal about these two objects. The data are released models, whose sizes and
token counts developers chose, and the experiments run to choose them. Our organizing idea is that optimization cuts
both ways for measurement. A developer that minimizes cost places its models on the expansion path, where they carry
almost no information about the curvature of the technology. The same optimization makes its choices informative about
its objectives: the further a model lies from the path that minimizes training cost, the more the developer must value
something that training cost leaves out. Designed experiments break the circle: they supply the technology, which then
turns choices into revealed objectives.

\textit{Theory.}---Because training compute is multiplicative in parameters and data, isocost lines are straight lines
in logs, and cost minimization equates the output elasticities of the two inputs, $\varepsilon_N$ and $\varepsilon_D$.
An interior optimum therefore requires the elasticity of substitution between parameters and data, $\sigma$, to lie
between zero and one (Lemma~\ref{lemma:sigma}). In logs a Cobb--Douglas isoquant is itself a straight line, so cost
has an interior minimum on an isoquant only where the isoquant is more curved than that. The U-shaped IsoFLOP profiles
that practitioners fit, loss against model size at fixed compute, are the empirical content of this restriction. The
ratio $w \equiv \varepsilon_N/\varepsilon_D$ measures what else the developer values. A developer stops adding
parameters when their marginal product equals their full marginal cost, which includes whatever it loses when a model
of given quality is larger; tokens cost only training. Under any objective that rewards lower loss, and ignoring data
costs and size-dependent FLOP prices, $w-1$ is therefore the marginal value of compactness at given quality divided by
training expenditure (Proposition~\ref{prop:wedge}). We report it as a share of lifetime cost, $s \equiv (w-1)/w$. For
a developer that serves its own models, $s$ is the planned serving share of lifetime cost. For a developer whose users
bear the serving cost, it is the value the developer places on compactness, through adoption, memory tiers or latency.

The same geometry limits what optimizing developers' data can reveal. On the expansion path, where parameters and data
are functions of compute alone, the data identify the path and the loss--compute frontier but the curvature of the
technology only through functional form (Proposition~\ref{prop:ident}). Off the path, the information about the
compute-optimal ratio of tokens to parameters, $M^*$, is second order in developers' allocation errors, and the
information about curvature is fourth order: curvature affects loss only through the loss from misallocation, which is
itself second order. The better developers optimize, the less their data reveal. This binds in compute-optimal ladders
run by a single laboratory; released models lie far off the path, and their heterogeneous values of compactness supply
variation that is chosen rather than designed. Designed experiments restore identification without a functional form.
With $\sigma^*$ the elasticity of substitution on the expansion path, $1/\sigma^*-1$ equals the curvature of the
IsoFLOP profile in log model size at its minimum, divided by twice the slope of the loss--compute frontier in log
compute (Proposition~\ref{prop:modelfree}). Along an IsoFLOP line, loss changes only through the input mix, and the
frontier slope converts that curvature into a proportion of the marginal product, so neither the irreducible loss nor
the functional form enters.

\textit{The technology.}---We apply this estimator to the IsoFLOP experiments of three laboratories, Chinchilla
\citep{hoffmann2022training}, Llama~3 \citep{grattafiori2024llama} and Marin's ladders on three corpora
\citep{marin2026ladders}, and to the near-factorial Farseer sweep \citep{li2025predictableb}. It gives a common
$\sigma^*$ of about 0.70, with a 95 percent confidence interval of $[0.67, 0.72]$ and no detectable heterogeneity
across designs. Parametric fits that free the outer curvature of the loss function come close to this value, while the
standard Chinchilla form overstates $\sigma^*$ on Llama~3's profiles. The level of the technology does not travel: the
compute-optimal ratio $M^*$ differs by more than an order of magnitude across designs, and the allocation exponent,
which governs how compute-optimal model size grows with compute, also differs across designs and published laws. Our
own pre-registered experiment trains the same architectures on two corpora of different quality with a shared
tokenizer. \textcolor{red}{[TBD-m9: one-sentence headline---model-free and flexible-form $\sigma^*$ by corpus; whether

\textit{What over-training reveals.}---We invert developers' first-order conditions on a clean inference-demand sample
of 77 open-weight base models released in 2023--2025, which excludes research suites, mixture-of-experts models, and
distilled or pruned models. The inversion needs two features of the technology, the zero point $M^*(C)$ at the model's
own compute $C$ and the curvature $1/\sigma^*-1$, because in the fitted families $\ln w = (1/\sigma^*-1)\ln[M/M^*(C)]$
with $M \equiv D/N$. Under our reference technology, the Chinchilla data fitted with the outer curvature free, the
median model is trained as if compactness were worth three-quarters of lifetime cost ($s = 0.75$), and almost every
model is over-trained relative to the training-optimal path. For developers that publish their own scaling laws, the
median under their own technology is 0.61, slightly below the reference for the same models. The trend is more robust
than the level. Among production-scale open-weight releases, the compute-weighted share under the reference rose from
0.04 in 2019--2022 to 0.82 in 2025, and it rises under every technology we consider.

These objects matter for economic questions (Section~\ref{sec:econ}). The allocation exponent governs forecasts of
data demand: across its estimates, compute-optimal data demand grows by a factor of 2.0 to 2.8 a year at the current
growth rate of compute. The curvature $\sigma^*$, by contrast, barely affects the cost of a binding cap on unique
training data; how quickly repeated data lose value matters far more \citep{muennighoff2023scaling,
villalobos2022run}.

\textit{What is robust.}---The model-free $\sigma^*$ is stable across the annealed designs of three laboratories. It
is lower, about 0.51, on the small-scale ladder of \citet{porian2024resolving}, whose runs were not annealed, and
parametric estimates from small sweeps without IsoFLOP profiles are heterogeneous. The sign of over-training is also
robust. For any technology with U-shaped IsoFLOP profiles, a model is over-trained exactly when its $M$ exceeds
$M^*(C)$, so the sign needs only bounds on $M^*$ beyond the largest designed budget (Proposition~\ref{prop:pi}). With
$M^*$ anchored at the largest IsoFLOP budgets and its drift bounded by published expansion paths, $w > 1$ is
identified for 86 percent of the clean sample and $w < 1$ for none. The level of $s$ is not robust. It depends on
$M^*$ at frontier scale, which no public design observes, and the median share ranges from 0.17 to 0.85 across the
technologies we specify ex ante. Inside Farseer's design, where extrapolation in tokens per parameter can be checked,
parametric forms understate $w$ at high $M$; beyond the designs in compute, nothing is observed. Open-weight models
are more over-trained than closed ones at given compute, and with few developers in the sample, serving one's own
models does not significantly predict over-training, so for developers that do not serve their models we read $s$ only
as a value of compactness. We treat $s$ as ordinal and conditional on a technology.

\textit{Related literature.}---\citet{hao2026theory} model a profit-maximizing developer that pays for training and
serving, and derive the elasticity of substitution implied by the Chinchilla form. They solve the developer's problem
under a Leontief approximation, which rules out over-training. Proposition~\ref{prop:wedge} inverts the first-order
conditions of such a problem when $\sigma > 0$ and extends it to developers that do not bear serving costs.
\citet{sardana2024beyond}, \citet{bian2025scaling} and \citet{roberts2026test} solve the forward problem: optimal size
and data given inference demand, latency constraints or repeated sampling at test time. We solve the inverse problem;
their mechanisms are sources of the value of compactness it recovers. \citet{kricheli2026tokens} show that designs
with a fixed ratio of tokens to parameters leave scaling-law parameters ill-conditioned when the two exponents are
close. Proposition~\ref{prop:ident} shows that on a compute-optimal path the same degeneracy arises whatever the
exponents, because equal decay rates of the two loss terms are what compute optimality means, and it gives the rate at
which allocation errors restore information. \citet{mertens2026secret} estimate developer-specific compute efficiency
from cross-developer regressions, and \citet{whitfill2025note} shows that observational estimates of algorithmic
progress are subject to selection bias. Online Appendix~\ref{app:obs} takes our framework to such observational data.

In industrial organization, the observation that optimizing firms facing common prices generate data that do not
identify the technology goes back to \citet{marschak1944random}. \citet{bond2005adjustment} show that identification
then requires variation in relative prices, adjustment costs or optimization errors, and
\citet{ackerberg2015identification} and \citet{gandhi2020identification} study the consequences for proxy and
first-order-condition estimators. Language-model training is the cleanest case, because FLOP accounting gives every
developer the same log-cost weights. We add the rates at which information returns with allocation errors, the
design-theoretic orders of \citet{box1959design} generated here by economic wedges, and a designed-experiment
estimator that needs no functional form. Our inversion follows the production approach of
\citet{deloecker2012markups}, but $w$ is not a markup: it prices model size beyond its cost in training. Its
elasticities come from experiments rather than from estimation on the same choices, which avoids the circularity
stressed by \citet{doraszelski2021reexamining} at the cost of external validity. The critiques of
\citet{bond2020unpleasant} and \citet{raval2023testing}, on the output concept and factor-biased technology, carry
over, and Section~\ref{sec:wedge} takes them up.
