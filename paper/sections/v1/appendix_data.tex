\section{Data Construction}\label{app:data}

% Table and figure numbers (B1, B2, ...) come from \appendix in main.tex.

This appendix documents the samples summarized in Table~\ref{tab:data}: their sources, the conventions we impose,
the corrections we make to source data, and the sample restrictions. A single script per module regenerates every
sample from the raw files with fixed seeds, and the replication package lists the download commands. The snapshots of
living databases are from September 23 and 24, 2026.

\subsection{Public Sweeps}\label{app:sweeps}

\paragraph{Common conventions.}
For every sweep we build a run-level panel with the fields $(N,D,L)$, a cluster identifier and the derived
quantities $M=D/N$ and $C=6ND$. We use $C=6ND$ even when a sweep reports a more detailed FLOP count, so that compute
means the same thing across sweeps. The sweeps do not share a parameter-count convention, a tokenizer or an output
measure (Table~\ref{tab:app_conventions}). We keep each sweep's own conventions and compare only unit-free objects
across sweeps. The exponents $\alpha$ and $\beta$ and the derived $a$, $\gamma$ and $\sigma$ are invariant to the unit
of loss. They are not invariant to the parameter-count convention, which Section~\ref{sec:tech:measurement} treats as a
measurement problem. The asymptote $E$, the levels $A$ and $B$, and the compute-optimal ratio $M^{*}$ are not
comparable across rows.

\paragraph{Design statistic.}
The off-path spread $\mathrm{sd}(\ln M\,|\,\ln C)$ in Table~\ref{tab:data} is the residual standard deviation of an OLS
regression of $\ln M$ on $\ln(6ND)$ over the estimation sample. Figure~\ref{fig:app_designs} plots the six public
designs with run-level losses against their isocost lines. Farseer is a near-factorial grid in $(\ln N,\ln D)$ with
$\mathrm{corr}(\ln N,\ln D)=-0.04$. The Chinchilla runs combine nine IsoFLOP profiles with off-profile streaks at
fixed $D$. Gadre et al.\ and the OLMo ladder are grids in size and token multiplier. DataDecide's final checkpoints
all lie at $D\approx 85$--$110\,N$, so only the intermediate checkpoints spread the design.

\paragraph{Farseer.}
The file \texttt{1222\_full.csv} of \citet{li2025predictableb} contains 404 runs: 25 model sizes, each trained on up to
19 token budgets with one data recipe and with hyperparameters set by the Step Law rules. $N$ excludes both of the
model's untied 65,536-row embedding matrices; we verified that the reported $N$ including embeddings exceeds $N$ by
exactly $131{,}072\,h$ for hidden size $h$. The output is English bits per character on the authors' validation set
(the average of its web, paper and book subsets), which is the output they fit. Three quirks required care. The column
labeled \texttt{D/N} takes five values unrelated to $D/N$, so we recompute $M$ from $D$ and $N$ (range 0.31--2,570).
The file's compute column includes attention FLOPs, at 6.6--8.5 FLOP per parameter per token, and we use $6ND$
instead. Four $(N,D)$ pairs were run twice, and we keep both copies.

\paragraph{Gadre et al.}
The 104 runs of \citet{gadre2024language} are 34 on C4, 35 on RedPajama and 35 on RefinedWeb. $N$ includes embeddings,
and $D=20\times\text{multiplier}\times N$ exactly. Our main output is the C4 validation loss (174 million tokens), on
which every model is evaluated, because tests of neutral data quality need a common output. In-distribution Paloma
losses serve as a robustness check. The authors' token-level confidence intervals imply a sampling standard error of
$\ln L$ of only 0.0016. The residual standard deviation of a fitted Chinchilla form is 0.023--0.030, so the residuals
reflect misspecification rather than evaluation noise.

\paragraph{OLMo ladder.}
We take the final logged step of 30 runs: five sizes trained to 0.5, 1, 2, 5 and 10 times the Chinchilla token budget,
plus five reruns \citep{bhagia2024establishing}. $N$ is OLMo's count, which excludes only the input embedding; we
verified it against the ladder code. $D$ is the logged token count at the final step. The output is C4-en validation
cross-entropy, and task bits per byte serve as a robustness output. Reruns are clustered with their originals.

\paragraph{Muennighoff et al.}
We parse 229 final losses from the authors' notebook with their own code. The aggregated third-party file mislabels
the tokens-per-epoch and epoch columns, so we rebuild $N$, $D$ and the unique tokens $U$ from the model names. The
primary sample is the 33 single-epoch runs ($D=U$). A robustness variant drops two runs with $D/N<0.4$, following
Besiroglu et al.'s rule for Chinchilla. The parameter-count convention in the authors' map is not documented.

\paragraph{DataDecide.}
The DataDecide release \citep{magnusson2025datadecide} crosses 25 data recipes with 14 model sizes (4M to 1.2B
parameters, excluding the input embedding) and three seeds per size (five at 1B), for 1,100 runs. $D$ is
step$\times$batch$\times$2,048, using the batch sizes in the authors' code, and the output is C4-en validation
cross-entropy. We keep 21,888 checkpoints with $D/N\ge5$ that lie at or before the end of the full schedule. The final
checkpoints have $D\approx85$--$110\,N$, and even with checkpoints $\mathrm{corr}(\ln N,\ln D)=0.91$. The checkpoints
that break the collinearity are evaluated before the cosine schedule has finished, and schedule progress is itself a
function of $D/N$. The levels of the fitted technology are therefore not interpretable, and we use DataDecide only for
comparisons across recipes, which share sizes, steps and schedules. This rests on the untestable assumption that the
unfinished schedule affects all recipes alike.

\paragraph{Sweeps we did not use.}
The Hugging Face release of \citet{kricheli2026tokens} contains no validation losses, only per-epoch training losses
from small multi-epoch runs, so we report no estimates from it. We did not process the open-sci-ref collection of
reference runs on eight corpora, which would allow a second multi-recipe neutrality test.

\subsection{Chinchilla Extraction}\label{app:chinchilla}

The Epoch AI digitization of Figure~4 of \citet{hoffmann2022training} contains 245 runs \citep{besiroglu2024chinchilla}.
$N$ is the total parameter count and $C$ is read off the figure's horizontal axis, so $D=C/(6N)$ is constructed. The
loss is MassiveText validation loss in nats per token, read from a 256-color map and hence quantized to about
$\pm0.01$. Besiroglu et al.\ drop the five highest-loss runs, all from the $10^{19}$ IsoFLOP budget, and we do the same
($n=240$). Four of the five have $D/N<0.4$; the fifth has 0.40. The five dropped runs lie in the data-starved corner
of the design; Section~\ref{sec:tech:chinchilla} shows how the results change when they are kept.

We reconstruct the nine nominal IsoFLOP budgets, $6\times10^{18}$ to $3\times10^{21}$ FLOP, for cluster-robust inference.
Digitized $\log_{10}C$ sits systematically 0.018 below the nominal budgets. We estimate this offset as the median
deviation of runs within 0.1 of a budget, remove it, and assign every run to its nearest budget. A run belongs to an
IsoFLOP profile if its corrected $\log_{10}C$ is within 0.045 of the budget. This rule gives 137 profile runs of 245
(132 of 240). The remaining runs form streaks of fixed $D$ in Hoffmann et al.'s scatter. In the 240-run sample, 33
percent of the variance of centered $(\ln N,\ln D)$ lies in the direction transverse to the expansion path. The
124-row \texttt{ml\_scalefit} Chinchilla series in the open IsoFLOP compilation of \citet{czech2026problems} is a
snapped subset of the same digitization rather than an independent source, so we do not use it.

\subsection{Laboratory IsoFLOP Profiles and Hyperparameter Sweeps}\label{app:isoflop}

\paragraph{Llama 3.}
The 133 points of Meta's IsoFLOP figure \citep{grattafiori2024llama} come from the digitization of
\citet{czech2026llama3isoflop}, distributed in the compilation of \citet{czech2026problems}; we checked that the two
agree row by row. There are ten budgets from $6\times10^{18}$ to $10^{22}$ FLOP. $D$ is read off the figure and
$N=C/(6D)$ uses the nominal budget, which assumes that Meta's budgets are $6ND$. The loss units are not stated; the
losses lie between 0.69 and 0.93.

\paragraph{Marin.}
The Marin project's scaling ladders \citep{marin2026ladders} train a Llama-2-style architecture on three corpora
(Comma, DCLM and Nemotron-CC): 85, 85 and 88 runs on seven or eight budgets from $1.8\times10^{18}$ to
$3\times10^{20}$ FLOP. The output is the macro-averaged Paloma loss. The nominal budgets are three times the forward
FLOPs and differ from $6ND$ by $-7$ to $+35$ percent. The difference is strongly negatively correlated with $N$ within
a budget, so FLOP accounting, as well as the finite IsoFLOP grid, affects IsoFLOP-parabola estimates on these data.
We also use, with caveats, the checkpoint-interpolated FineWeb/C4 profiles of \citet{li2025misfitting} (176 points).

\paragraph{Porian et al.}
The released results of \citet{porian2024resolving} contain 975 runs (772 on RefinedWeb, 203 on OpenWebText2): 264
base, 61 tuned, 566 hyperparameter-sweep and 84 seed runs of 16 architectures (width 96--1,504, depth 3--30, vocabulary
50,432, sequence length 2,048). We count parameters and FLOPs without the output head (Kaplan), with it (standard) and
with attention FLOPs (attention), as the authors do, and add a fourth count that also includes the input and
positional embeddings (total). Our re-implementation of the authors' IsoFLOP pipeline reads each run's loss along its
loss curve at twelve budgets $1.25\times10^{16}\times2^{i}$ ($i=0,\dots,11$), takes the minimum over runs for each $N$,
locates the minimizing $N$ by spline interpolation, and reproduces the published path estimates to within 0.003.

\paragraph{Step Law.}
The dense-model file of \citet{li2025predictablea} contains 1,911 runs in 17 $(N,D)$ cells. $N$ takes five values
between 215M and 1.07B non-embedding parameters, $D$ ranges from 4B to 100B tokens, and $M$ ranges from 19 to 466. Each
cell has a grid of up to 12 learning rates and 10 batch sizes; one cell has only 5 learning rates and 47 runs. Batch
size is in sequences of 2,048 tokens, and the output is the smoothed final training loss of a single-epoch run. We
classify 9.5 percent of runs as diverged: those whose log loss exceeds the cell minimum by more than 0.30.

\subsection{The Controlled Two-Lab Experiment}\label{app:sweep}

\paragraph{Corpora and tokenizer.}
Lab ``edu'' trains on FineWeb-Edu and lab ``web'' on FineWeb \citep{penedo2024fineweb}. We use the first two
files of each sample-10BT release. The first 20,000 documents of each corpus are held out for validation (24.6 million
FineWeb-Edu tokens and 16.7 million FineWeb tokens). The remaining 1.76 billion and 1.70 billion training tokens exceed
the largest budget (800 million), so no run repeats data. A byte-level BPE tokenizer \citep{sennrich2016neural} with
8,192 tokens, including an end-of-text token that separates documents, is trained on about 300 million characters of
each training corpus. Because both labs share the tokenizer, losses are in the same units. On the validation sets a
token covers 3.89 bytes of FineWeb-Edu and 3.74 bytes of FineWeb. We use these ratios to convert losses to bits per
byte, $L/(3.89\ln2)$ and $L/(3.74\ln2)$, which expresses losses per byte of raw text and so removes the difference in how well the shared tokenizer
compresses the two corpora.

\paragraph{Architecture and input counts.}
The models are decoder-only transformers with RMSNorm pre-normalization \citep{zhang2019root}, rotary position
embeddings \citep{su2024roformer}, bias-free linear layers, a four-times-wide GELU feed-forward block and an input
embedding tied to the output projection. Width $d$ determines depth $d/64$ and the number of heads $d/64$, so the head
dimension is always 64. Table~\ref{tab:app_sweepdesign} lists the eight sizes. We record both parameter counts. The
non-embedding count is $L(12d^2+2d)+d$ for $L$ layers, and the tied embedding adds $8{,}192\,d$, which is 73 percent of
all parameters at $d=128$ and 10 percent at $d=640$. This range makes the counting convention matter far more than in
production models, which is the input-measurement problem of \citet{pearce2024reconciling} and
\citet{porian2024resolving}. Training FLOP per token, including attention and the output projection, is within 5
percent of $6N_{\text{total}}$ at every width but 1.14 to 3.83 times $6N_{\text{non-emb}}$. Initialization is Gaussian
with standard deviation $1/\sqrt{\text{fan-in}}$. Residual-branch output projections are scaled down by
$1/\sqrt{2L}$, and the embedding has standard deviation 0.02.

\paragraph{Optimization and schedule.}
All runs use AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019decoupled} with decay rates 0.9 and 0.99 for the first and second moments,
stability constant $10^{-8}$, decoupled weight decay 0.1 and gradient clipping at norm 1. The sequence length is 256 and the batch is 64 sequences (16,384
tokens). The learning rate warms up linearly over 250 steps (4.1 million tokens) to its peak and then stays constant on
the trunk. For each budget $D_k$ the run takes $S_k=\lceil D_k/16{,}384\rceil$ steps. At step $0.8S_k$ the model and
optimizer state are copied, and the copy is trained to $S_k$ with learning rate
$\mathrm{lr}^{*}\bigl(1-\sqrt{(s-0.8S_k)/(0.2S_k)}\bigr)$. The largest budget is the trunk's own cooldown. This
warmup-stable-decay design with cooldown branches follows \citet{hagele2024scaling}, who find that it performs on par
with cosine schedules tuned to each budget. One trunk thus delivers a whole column of the $(N,D)$ design, and every endpoint is
annealed. Endpoints of one width share the trunk up to their branch points. Their noise is therefore positively correlated
across budgets, and inference should cluster by width.

\paragraph{Learning-rate calibration.}
Before the main grid, we trained widths 128, 320 and 512 at four peak learning rates ($1$, $2$, $4$ and
$8\times10^{-3}$) for 50 million FineWeb-Edu tokens (12 runs) and evaluated them on FineWeb-Edu. For each width, the
optimal rate is the minimum of a quadratic in the log learning rate, allowed to extrapolate at most 0.35 log points
beyond the tested range. The optima are $5.75\times10^{-3}$, $2.47\times10^{-3}$ and $1.66\times10^{-3}$. A log-linear
fit gives $\mathrm{lr}^{*}(d)=3.07\times10^{-3}(d/256)^{-0.90}$, which we apply to both labs and every budget. The
learning rate is thus a flexible input optimized over width at one budget and for one lab only. By the argument of
Section~\ref{sec:tech:measurement}, any remaining inefficiency that varies with $D$ or with the lab would bias the data exponent or
the measured factor bias. The batch size is the same at every width.

\paragraph{Common random numbers, seeds and evaluation.}
Within a lab, every width in the main grid uses initialization seed 0 and the same permutation of training sequences,
so differences across $(N,D)$ cells are not driven by different data draws. The two labs necessarily see different
data. The seed replicates rerun widths 128, 256 and 384 with two further seeds, each changing both the initialization
and the data order, to budgets of 50, 100 and 200 million FineWeb-Edu tokens (18 endpoints). With the main grid they give
three replicates per cell, which measure the variance of pure noise in the output. Every endpoint is evaluated on the
first 1,048,576 tokens (4,096 sequences of 256) of \emph{both} validation sets. Each lab's models therefore have an
in-distribution and a cross-distribution loss, and the two labs can be compared on a common output. For each endpoint
we record $N$ under both conventions, the tokens trained, compute under three conventions, both validation losses and
the trunk's training loss. Training curves of the constant-learning-rate trunks are logged every 1,000 steps.

\paragraph{Hardware.}
All models were trained in MLX \citep{hannun2023mlx} on a single Apple M5 Max. Parameters and optimizer moments are
kept in 32-bit floating point, and every matrix product is computed in bfloat16 by casting the weights on the fly, so
that small updates late in the cooldown are not lost to rounding. \textcolor{red}{[TBD-m9: completed endpoints per lab,
seed replicates completed, any diverged or failed runs, and total wall-clock hours.]}

\subsection{Choice Data: Samples A and B}\label{app:choices}

\paragraph{Sample A (Epoch universe).}
We start from the 2,070 language-model rows of the Epoch AI database \citep{epochai2026data} (snapshot of September
23, 2026) and exclude 1,728 of them: derived models such as fine-tunes and post-training updates (531); rows without
$N$ (361) or released outside January 2019--September 2026 (234); rows whose token count cannot be recovered (224);
multimodal and non-text models, whose $D$ would include other tokens (133); benchmark-scale research models with unique
corpora below $5\times10^{9}$ tokens (100); encoders and encoder-decoders (58); post-trained variants identified by name
(16); mixture-of-experts models without an active-parameter count (27); rows with implausible epochs ($>20$) or
$D/N>30{,}000$ (15); and 29 manual exclusions with recorded reasons, such as post-training updates listed as base models
(Llama~3.3 70B), systems-paper benchmark runs and leaked GPT-4 figures. This leaves 342 models.

$D$ is the dataset size times the number of epochs, with missing epochs set to 1. Epoch records some older datasets in
words. Where Epoch's notes indicate a word count, we convert it to tokens, using operation-counted compute when it is
informative and a ratio of 4/3 tokens per word otherwise (7 rows). Where the dataset size is missing but compute is operation-counted
and at least ``likely'', we set $D=C/(6N)$ (67 rows). Six token counts are corrected from primary sources, each
documented in Epoch's own notes. GPT-3 175B was trained on 300B tokens, and GLM-130B on 400B rather than 152B. For
Cerebras-GPT-13B (257B) and BloombergGPT (569B), Epoch's dataset size already counts tokens seen. Polyglot-Ko-12.8B saw
167B tokens rather than 96B words, and for GPT-SW3 3.5B we use 102B tokens. For mixture-of-experts models, active
parameters come from the name, Epoch's notes, or operation-counted compute. Eleven production-scale rows still have $D$
inconsistent with Epoch's operation-counted or reported compute ($|\ln C/(6ND)|>\ln1.5$). They are flagged, and the
trends in Section~\ref{sec:wedge} are robust to dropping them.

\paragraph{Sample B (verified open-weight base models).}
Sample B starts from the 148 base models in the ObsScaling evaluation file \citep{ruan2024observational}, with nine
documented corrections and fills. For example, OPT's $D$ is 300B tokens, not the 180B-token corpus, and TinyLlama v1.1 was
trained on 2T tokens, not 3T. We drop 17 models whose token counts are undisclosed (Mistral~7B and NeMo, Mixtral,
Jamba, DeciLM, Qwen1.5-110B, GPT-2) or ambiguous (InternLM2 7B and 20B), upcycled, duplicated or instruction-tuned.
We replace the deduplicated Pythia suite with the standard suite \citep{biderman2023pythia}. We then add 57 models
released in 2024--2026, whose token counts come from model cards or technical reports. For 47 of them, a regular
expression matches the training-token statement in the saved model card. This gives 188 base models in 73 families.
Excluding 15 code-specialized models gives the 173 general-purpose models, in 68 families, used in
Section~\ref{sec:wedge}.

For 143 of the 188 models, $N$ is the exact count of floating-point tensors in the Hugging Face checkpoint. Integer
buffers such as stored causal masks are excluded; they inflate the count of the smallest GPT-NeoX models by up to 36
percent. The 417M-parameter vision encoder of the multimodal Gemma~3 checkpoints is subtracted. For the remaining
models the reported size is used. Mixture-of-experts models enter with active parameters, for example 37B of 671B for
DeepSeek-V3. Embedding counts, from each model's configuration file, give the non-embedding $N$ that the Farseer
technology requires (156 models). Of the 119 Sample-B models that also appear in Epoch, 5 have token counts that
differ from Epoch's by more than 0.05 in $\log_{10}$. All five differences are documented; Phi-2, for instance, saw
1.4T tokens, whereas Epoch lists the 250B-token dataset. We flag distilled (10), synthetic-data-heavy (16),
continued-pretraining (8), multimodal (5), mixture-of-experts (13) and recurrent (6) models. The release date is
Epoch's publication date when matched and otherwise the developer's announcement or the Hub upload date.

\paragraph{Production-scale universe.}
Trends, rival explanations and the aggregate in Section~\ref{sec:wedge} use models trained with $6ND\ge10^{21}$ FLOP.
This universe applies the same threshold to both samples. It consists of Sample-A rows that are not speculative, not
code models and not matched to Sample B (176), plus the Sample-B general-purpose models above the threshold (159). The
total is 335 models released in 2019--2026, 271 of them open weight, from 94 developers after we merge name variants
(for example, all Alphabet units into Google).

\paragraph{Usage.}
For the Sample-B models with Hub metadata (164 models from 26 developers), we record 30-day and all-time downloads and
likes. Downloads are summed over the base repository and the developer's official post-trained releases, including
later post-training updates of the same base. From OpenRouter \citep{openrouter2026models} we record whether a model
(or an official post-trained version) is listed, the number of distinct providers across its listed releases, and the
minimum completion price. From LMArena \citep{lmarena2026leaderboard} we record the maximum vote count over all
published text leaderboards (46 matched models).

\paragraph{Units.}
Wedges are computed in each technology's own conventions. $N$ is the total count for the Chinchilla, Gadre et al.\
and Meta technologies and the non-embedding count for Farseer's headline row. $D$ is in each model's own tokens, while each technology
is in its sweep's tokens. A 20 percent difference in tokenizer efficiency moves $\ln w$ by about $\beta\times0.18\approx
0.07$. Under the reference technology, $\partial\ln w/\partial\ln D=\beta\approx0.37$ and
$\partial\ln w/\partial\ln N=-\alpha\approx-0.35$.

\subsection{Benchmark Panel}\label{app:obspanel}

The benchmark panel of Section~\ref{sec:obs} combines the ObsScaling base-model file with the Sloth data
\citep{maiapolo2024sloth}. Sloth adds base models absent from ObsScaling and fills missing scores. Five Sloth rows that
duplicate ObsScaling's deduplicated Pythia scores are dropped. Parameter counts are Hub floating-point tensor counts
where available (17 models were affected by the causal-mask correction), and reported token counts are corrected much
as in Sample B. The outputs are HellaSwag (10-shot), ARC-Challenge (25-shot) and Winogrande (5-shot) accuracies from the
same evaluation harness. We map accuracy to the log odds of above-chance success,
$q=\operatorname{logit}\bigl((\text{acc}-\text{chance})/(1-\text{chance})\bigr)$, with chance-adjusted accuracy
clipped to $[0.01,0.99]$; the composite is the logit of the mean chance-adjusted accuracy of the three tasks.
The main sample contains 128 dense models with $N$, $D$ and all three outputs, from 38 families and 22 developers,
released March 2021--July 2024. Mixture-of-experts models are excluded. The overlap sample ($N\le14$B, $D\le5$T)
has 90 models, and the 57 models inside the convex hull of the experimental designs form the common-support sample.

The panel's design is revealing. $C=6ND$ holds in every row by construction. Within families the standard deviation of
$\ln D$ is 0.42, against 1.29 for $\ln N$. Only 18 families (66 models) vary $D$ at all, and
$\mathrm{corr}(\ln N,\ln D)=0.46$. The experimental benchmarks are the OLMo ladder together
with the OLMo~2 7B and 13B targets, the 104 Gadre et al.\ models, the Pythia checkpoints and the final DataDecide
checkpoints. They were evaluated with their developers' own harnesses, so the correspondence between output formats is
part of the maintained null hypothesis in Section~\ref{sec:obs}.

\subsection{Algorithmic-Progress Data}\label{app:ho}

We re-implement line by line the sample construction of \citet{ho2024algorithmic} from their public notebooks, starting
from the 408-row sheet. We keep rows with numeric $N$ and $D$, not marked as excluded, outliers or uncertain, and with
at least one perplexity. We stack the rows by benchmark, drop four named systems ($\mu$P and LoRA fine-tunes), and keep the three
best-performing models per paper. This yields 231 observations from 144 papers: 103 on WikiText-103, 80 on Penn
Treebank and 48 on WikiText-2. $D$ is the dataset size in unique tokens, not tokens processed. Epochs are known for 155
of the 231 rows; we impute the rest by the median within bins of $\log_{10}D$, and set epochs to one for $D\ge10^{10}$.
The effective-data variant follows \citet{muennighoff2023scaling}. The output is log perplexity, mostly per word; its units
vary with each evaluation's tokenization.

For the allocative-efficiency analysis we build a separate sample of Epoch language models
\citep{epochai2026data}. Of the 792 models with $N$ and $C$, 544 report a dataset size and 454 are not fine-tunes. Of
these, 428 are dense. In 339 of them, $C/(6N)$ lies within a factor of three of dataset size times epochs, and 247 of
those were trained for at most four epochs. We set $D=C/(6N)$ so that $6ND=C$ exactly.
