\section{The Training Problem}\label{sec:framework}

This section writes a scaling law as a production technology with a known, multiplicative cost and states the three
results used throughout: duality, the requirement that interior compute optima have an elasticity of substitution below
one, and the inversion of a developer's first-order conditions. Proofs are in Online Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

\subsection{Technology and Cost}\label{sec:framework:tech}

A developer trains a language model with $N$ parameters on $D$ tokens (tokens processed, counting repetitions) and
measures its cross-entropy loss $L$, in nats per token, on a held-out corpus. Following \citet{hoffmann2022training},
with productivity terms added, we write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tech}
L=E+e^{-\omega}\Big[A\big(e^{\psi_N}N\big)^{-\alpha}+B\big(e^{\psi_D}D\big)^{-\beta}\Big]^{\kappa}e^{\epsilon},
\qquad A,B,\alpha,\beta,\kappa>0 .
\end{equation}
Here $E$ is the irreducible loss, and the bracket collects the losses from finite parameters and finite data.
Productivity is Hicks-neutral ($\omega$) or factor-augmenting ($\psi_N$ and $\psi_D$; a cleaner corpus raises $\psi_D$),
and $\epsilon$ is seed and evaluation noise. The developer knows its productivity up to the error of its own small-scale
experiments; Proposition~\ref{prop:wedge} shows what a gap between belief and technology does. The Chinchilla law has
$\kappa=1$ and no productivity terms. The outer exponent $\kappa$ scales both output elasticities and leaves the
isoquants unchanged, so it separates returns to scale from substitution; $\kappa=1$ is testable (Section~\ref{sec:tech}).
Where the noise enters matters only for estimation, and the information rates of Section~\ref{sec:ident} are the same
with noise on $\ln L$.

Lower-case letters denote logs, $n\equiv\ln N$, $d\equiv\ln D$ and $c\equiv\ln C$, and $y\equiv-\ln(L-E)$ is the log
output index. With $u\equiv A(e^{\psi_N}N)^{-\alpha}$ and $v\equiv B(e^{\psi_D}D)^{-\beta}$, the output elasticities are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:elast}
\varepsilon_N\equiv\frac{\partial y}{\partial n}=\frac{\kappa\alpha u}{u+v},\qquad
\varepsilon_D\equiv\frac{\partial y}{\partial d}=\frac{\kappa\beta v}{u+v},
\end{equation}
and neither depends on $\omega$, $\epsilon$ or $E$. When $\alpha=\beta$, reducible loss is a power of a constant-returns
CES aggregate of $N$ and $D$. When $\alpha\neq\beta$ the technology is not homothetic, but it is quasi-homothetic with
respect to the non-uniform scaling $(N,D)\mapsto(t^{\beta}N,t^{\alpha}D)$, which multiplies reducible loss by
$t^{-\kappa\alpha\beta}$ for every $t>0$ (Online Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}, Lemma~\ref{lem:geometry}). Every isoquant is
therefore a rescaled copy of every other, the compute-optimal mix drifts with compute as a power law, and all curvature
lies across the expansion path. This is why data on the path carry no information about curvature
(Section~\ref{sec:ident}).

Training a dense transformer takes about six floating-point operations (FLOPs) per parameter per token, so training
compute is $C\approx6ND$ \citep{kaplan2020scaling}. This is a FLOP-accounting approximation, not an identity: it omits
attention and embedding FLOPs, and FLOPs are not dollars. Proposition~\ref{prop:wedge} carries size-dependent FLOP
prices and data costs. In FLOP units the cost is multiplicative: both log-cost weights, $\partial c/\partial n$ and
$\partial c/\partial d$, equal one, and the implicit price of a parameter, $6D$, rises with the quantity of the other
input. Minimizing compute for a given loss therefore equates the output elasticities, $\varepsilon_N=\varepsilon_D$,
without the price data that the first-order-condition approaches of \citet{deloecker2012markups} and
\citet{gandhi2020identification} require: FLOP accounting supplies the cost shares. Isocosts are straight lines in logs,
$n+d=c-\ln6$.

In this language, iso-loss curves are isoquants, an IsoFLOP profile traces loss along an isocost, the compute-optimal
allocation $\big(N^*(C),D^*(C)\big)$ is a conditional factor demand, and the loss--compute frontier $L^*(C)$ is the
inverse of a cost function (Figure~\ref{fig:geometry}, panel~a). We write $M\equiv D/N$ for tokens per parameter and
$M^*(C)$ for its compute-optimal value. Isoquants, the elasticity of substitution, the compute-optimal allocation and the
wedge defined below are ordinal: they are unchanged by any strictly increasing transformation of the same output (loss,
bits per byte, or accuracy on a benchmark that is monotone in loss) and do not depend on $E$, $\omega$ or $\kappa$
(Online Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}, Lemma~\ref{lem:sigma}). The frontier elasticity and returns to scale are cardinal,
defined in units of reducible loss. Input units matter too: $M^*$ depends on the tokenizer and on whether embedding
parameters are counted, so it is comparable across studies only after harmonization (Section~\ref{sec:tech}).

Online Appendix Table~\ref{tab:app-dictionary} gives a full dictionary between scaling-law practice and production
economics. Three analogies break down. There are no market prices for parameters and tokens: FLOP accounting supplies
the log-cost weights, and differences in the price of a FLOP appear as wedges. Returns to scale measured in loss depend
on the cardinalization of output, so ``diminishing returns to compute'' has no unit-free content. And the inputs of a
training sweep are assigned by an experimenter, not chosen: an IsoFLOP sweep is designed variation in the input mix, not
a shock to relative prices, so the endogeneity problems of IO arise for released models, not for experiments.

\subsection{Duality}\label{sec:framework:duality}

\begin{proposition}[Duality]\label{prop:duality}
Let $\kappa=1$ in \eqref{eq:tech}, set productivity and noise to zero except in part (iii), and let
$a\equiv\beta/(\alpha+\beta)$, $\gamma\equiv\alpha\beta/(\alpha+\beta)$ and $G\equiv(\alpha A/\beta B)^{1/(\alpha+\beta)}$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] The allocation that minimizes $6ND$ subject to $L\le\bar L$, or maximizes $y$ at given $C$, satisfies
$\varepsilon_N=\varepsilon_D$. The conditional factor demands are $N^*(C)=G\,(C/6)^{a}$ and $D^*(C)=G^{-1}(C/6)^{1-a}$,
so $M^*(C)=G^{-2}(C/6)^{1-2a}$.
\item[(ii)] The loss--compute frontier and the cost function are $L^*(C)=E+K(C/6)^{-\gamma}$ and
$C^*(L)=6\,[K/(L-E)]^{1/\gamma}$, with $K\equiv\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\beta}AG^{-\alpha}$. Along the expansion path,
$\varepsilon_N=\varepsilon_D=\gamma$.
\item[(iii)] At given compute, Hicks-neutral productivity $\omega$ leaves the allocation unchanged, and factor-augmenting
productivity moves it only through the bias index $\chi\equiv\beta\psi_D-\alpha\psi_N$: $\ln M^*(C)$ falls by
$2\chi/(\alpha+\beta)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\noindent The proof is in Online Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} (Lemma~\ref{lem:alloc}). The proposition is Shephard--Uzawa
duality for the scaling law \citep{shephard1953cost,uzawa1964duality}: the technology, the conditional factor demands and
the cost function carry the same information, linked by $a$ and $\gamma$. With $\kappa\neq1$, part (i) is unchanged and
$\gamma$ is multiplied by $\kappa$. By part (ii), the elasticity of compute with respect to output, measured as $1/(L-E)$, is
$1/\gamma$ along the frontier; its inverse, $\gamma$, is the returns-to-scale measure that \citet{nerlove1963returns} estimated
from a cost function for electricity supply and found to vary with scale. Under
the parameters of \citet{besiroglu2024chinchilla}, $\gamma=0.178$: a one percent reduction in reducible loss requires
about 5.6 percent more compute.

Scaling studies estimate each of these objects. A fit of \eqref{eq:tech} to all runs (Approach~3 of
\citealp{hoffmann2022training}) is a primal estimator. A regression of the loss-minimizing $\ln N$ of each IsoFLOP profile
on $\ln C$ (their Approach~2) estimates the factor demand, and a frontier fitted through the same minima, which we call
the IsoFLOP-minima frontier, estimates the cost function; their Approach~1 reports the minimizing $N$ and $D$ as well as
the minimal loss, so it estimates both. Gaps among these estimates test duality \citep{klump2007factor}; Online
Appendix~\ref{app:additional} reports such tests. The minima of IsoFLOP profiles are outcomes of inputs that an
experimenter assigned, not choices.

\subsection{Substitution}\label{sec:framework:sigma}

The Hicks elasticity of substitution between parameters and data depends on the input mix only through the ratio of the
output elasticities, $w\equiv\varepsilon_N/\varepsilon_D=\alpha u/(\beta v)$, which we call the wedge. For every member
of the family \eqref{eq:tech},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigmaw}
\sigma(w)=\frac{1+w}{1+\alpha+w(1+\beta)},\qquad \sigma^*\equiv\sigma(1)=\frac{2}{2+\alpha+\beta},
\end{equation}
which follows from the formula of \citet{hao2026theory} (Online Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}, Lemma~\ref{lem:sigma}). The
local elasticity lies between $1/(1+\alpha)$ and $1/(1+\beta)$ and equals $\sigma^*$ on the compute-optimal path, where
$w=1$. With two inputs, the Hicks, Allen--Uzawa and Morishima elasticities coincide \citep{blackorby1989real}. Under the
\citet{besiroglu2024chinchilla} parameters ($\alpha=0.348$, $\beta=0.366$), $\sigma^*=0.737$, and $\sigma$ varies only
between 0.732 and 0.742 over all input mixes.

\begin{lemma}[Interior compute optima require $0<\sigma<1$]\label{lemma:sigma}
Let $y=F(n,d)$ be any twice continuously differentiable technology with $F_n,F_d>0$, and let $C=6ND$. At an allocation
with $\varepsilon_N=\varepsilon_D$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] the second-order condition for minimizing compute at given output holds strictly if and only if $0<\sigma<1$
there;
\item[(ii)] if $\sigma>1$ or $\sigma<0$ there, the allocation minimizes rather than maximizes output along its isocost;
if $\sigma>1$ everywhere, compute-optimal training is a corner solution.
\end{enumerate}
Every member of the family \eqref{eq:tech} has $\sigma<1$ at every input mix, so the first-order condition in
Proposition~\ref{prop:duality} is sufficient.
\end{lemma}

\noindent The proof is in Online Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} (Lemma~\ref{lem:soc} and Corollary~\ref{cor:complements}).
With a linear cost $q_NN+q_DD$, isocosts are straight lines in levels, and any convex isoquant ($\sigma>0$) gives an
interior tangency. With $C=6ND$ they are straight lines in logs, so the isoquant must be convex in logs, which requires
$\sigma<1$. Because the implicit price of each input rises with the quantity of the other, shifting toward one input
raises its own price, and that pays only if the inputs are complements in this sense.

The lemma's observable implication is that IsoFLOP profiles, loss against $\ln N$ at fixed compute, are U-shaped with an
interior minimum, as the designs of Section~\ref{sec:tech} show wherever their grids bracket the minimum. That
$\sigma^*<1$ is implied by this fact and imposed by every functional form we fit; it is not a finding. Its level is:
Proposition~\ref{prop:modelfree} turns the curvature of the U into $\sigma^*$ without a functional form. And $\sigma>0$
is what makes over-training a sensible response to a value of compactness. In the Leontief limit of \citet{hao2026theory} the optimum sits at the kink whatever that value, whereas
for $0<\sigma<1$ parameters can be traded for tokens at a cost governed by $\sigma^*$ (Figure~\ref{fig:geometry},
panel~b).

\subsection{What Over-Training Reveals}\label{sec:framework:wedge}

Most language models released since 2023 lie far from the expansion path, with $M$ well above $M^*(C)$
(Section~\ref{sec:wedge}). A developer that minimized training cost would have $w=1$; the next result says what a wedge
above one reveals about the developer's objective.

\begin{proposition}[What over-training reveals]\label{prop:wedge}
A developer chooses $(N,D)$ to maximize $V\big(L(N,D),N\big)-X_T-c_DD$. Here $V$ is any objective that falls with loss
and may depend on size directly; $X_T\equiv c_T(N)\,6ND$ is training expenditure at the (shadow) price $c_T(N)$ of a
training FLOP, whose size elasticity is $\delta$; and $c_D\ge0$ is a cost per token. Let
$m_N\equiv-(\partial V/\partial\ln N)_L/X_T$ be the value of compactness at fixed quality per unit of training
expenditure, and $m_D\equiv c_DD/X_T$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] At an interior optimum with no binding constraint,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gw}
w=\frac{\varepsilon_N}{\varepsilon_D}=\frac{1+\delta+m_N}{1+m_D}.
\end{equation}
With $\delta=m_D=0$, $w-1=m_N$, and the expenditure share $s\equiv(w-1)/w$ equals $m_N/(1+m_N)$. The wedge is computed
from $(N,D)$ and the technology alone; it does not depend on $\omega$, $\epsilon$, $E$ or $\kappa$.
\item[(ii)] (Serving.) Let $\delta=m_D=0$, and let the developer bear a share $\lambda$ of the expenditure $X_S$ on serving
$T$ tokens over the model's life, at $2N$ FLOPs per token and $p$ times the price of a training FLOP, with $T$ independent
of size at given loss. Then $w=1+\lambda pT/(3D)$, $w-1=\lambda X_S/X_T$, and $s=\lambda X_S/(X_T+\lambda X_S)$ is the
internalized serving share of lifetime expenditure.
\item[(iii)] (Constraints and misspecification.) A binding memory, tier or latency cap multiplies the right side of
\eqref{eq:gw} by $1+\nu$ with $\nu\ge0$; a binding cap on tokens adds $\mu\ge0$ to its denominator; and logit distillation
from a teacher with $N_T$ parameters is a data cost with $m_D=N_T/(3N)$. If the developer's first-order conditions involve
a technology $L'$ other than the econometrician's $L$ (its own productivity, a law it believes, or the output it values),
the measured wedge is $\hat w=w\,w_L/w_{L'}$, which equals $we^{-\chi}$ under factor-augmenting productivity.
\item[(iv)] (Family token budgets.) If a family trains models of sizes $N_1,\dots,N_K$ on one token budget $D$, member
wedges differ only through the technology: $\ln w_i-\ln w_j=-\alpha(\ln N_i-\ln N_j)$. If the sizes are chosen at the
margin and there is no data cost, the family's first-order conditions identify one number,
$\sum_i\pi_im_{N,i}=\bar w_H-1$, where $\bar w_H$ is the compute-weighted harmonic mean of the member wedges and the weight
$\pi_i$ is proportional to member $i$'s share of the family's training compute divided by $w_i$. If the sizes come from a
menu, the wedges carry no information about the $m_{N,i}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\noindent The proof is in Online Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} (Propositions~\ref{prop:A-wedge} and~\ref{prop:A-family}),
which also treats demand that responds to size and minimum-size and time-to-train constraints. The developer adds
parameters until their marginal value in loss equals their marginal cost: training expenditure plus whatever it loses
when the model is larger at the same quality, such as the cost of serving it, adoption among users who cannot run it,
latency or a memory tier. Tokens cost only training and data. The ratio of the two first-order conditions therefore puts
the value of compactness in the numerator, and an econometrician who sees only training cost observes a wedge: the
developer acts as if parameters were $w$ times as expensive, relative to tokens, as training accounting says.

What $w-1$ measures depends on the developer's objective; Online Appendix Table~\ref{tab:app-wedge-cases} tabulates
eleven cases. Three models of conduct matter most. For a developer that serves its own model
($\lambda=1$), $s$ is planned serving expenditure as a share of lifetime expenditure, with no conversion of FLOPs into
dollars; a count of tokens, $T=3D(w-1)/(\lambda p)$, needs the price ratio $p$, which is likely above one because
decoding is often bound by memory bandwidth. Compute proportional to $N$ that is planned at training time, such as
reinforcement-learning rollouts, and test-time scaling \citep{roberts2026test} enter $X_S$ in the same way. For an
open-weight developer whose users bear the serving cost, $w-1$ is the value of compactness through adoption. Under a
binding tier, memory or latency cap \citep{bian2025scaling}, $w-1$ only bounds $m_N$ from above. Section~\ref{sec:wedge}
tests the three readings as a problem of conduct \citep{berry2014identification,backus2021common,duarte2024testing}. We
therefore call $s$ the share of the value of compactness, and planned serving expenditure only for developers that serve
their models.

The other sources of a wedge have known signs (Online Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}, Proposition~\ref{prop:A-wedge}(iv)).
Caps, a price of a FLOP that rises with size, and parameter-augmenting productivity ($\chi<0$) raise the measured wedge.
Data costs, caps on tokens, distillation \citep{busbridge2025distillation}, minimum-size floors and data-augmenting
productivity ($\chi>0$) lower it. Serving demand cannot produce a wedge below one as long as serving cost rises with size.
Under the \citet{besiroglu2024chinchilla} parameters, Gopher has $w=0.36$, which reveals a binding data constraint, better data, or optimization against another technology, plausibly the law of
\citet{kaplan2020scaling}. Heterogeneity in these wedges is what moves released models off the expansion path, so it is
also the variation that could identify the technology from observational data, the counterpart of the relative-price
variation of \citet{bond2005adjustment} (Section~\ref{sec:ident}).

The construction parallels the production approach to markups \citep{deloecker2012markups}. The wedge is the ratio
statistic of \citet{raval2023testing} with FLOP-accounting weights; a ratio different from one, which Raval reads as
misspecification, is here the object of interest. The two classic critiques are the last case of part (iii):
factor-augmenting heterogeneity \citep{demirer2020production,raval2023testing} and a mismatch between the measured
output and the output the developer values \citep{bond2020unpleasant}. As in \citet{deridder2026hitchhiker}, levels are
more fragile than ranks.

Part (iv) matters because many families train every size on one token budget (Section~\ref{sec:wedge}). Within such a
family a small sibling's large wedge is fixed by the technology, so member-level wedges are not revealed preferences; the
family reveals a weighted average that puts less weight on its more over-trained members or, if sizes come from a menu
of tiers, nothing about compactness. A common budget under member-by-member choice would be a knife-edge, so its
prevalence suggests that $D$ is chosen at the family level.

In the $\kappa$ family the wedge has a closed form. For a model with compute $C$ and tokens per parameter $M$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wM}
\ln w=\Big(\frac1{\sigma^*}-1\Big)\ln\frac{M}{M^*(C)},
\end{equation}
where $M^*(C)$ is the compute-optimal ratio at the model's own compute (Online Appendix~\ref{app:proofs},
Lemma~\ref{lem:geometry} and Corollary~\ref{cor:suff}). The log wedge is the distance from the expansion path at equal
compute, scaled by curvature (Figure~\ref{fig:geometry}, panel~a). The technology supplies the zero point $M^*(C)$ and
the scale $1/\sigma^*-1$; at given compute, it ranks models by $M$. A model with 8 billion parameters trained on 15
trillion tokens (nominally Llama~3~8B, $M\approx1{,}875$) has $w\approx5.2$ under the \citet{besiroglu2024chinchilla}
parameters: its developer behaves as if making it one percent smaller at the same loss were worth 4.2 percent of the
training bill, and $s\approx0.81$. (Section~\ref{sec:wedge} estimates $w\approx8.4$ under Meta's own technology.) Such
estimates are only as good as
$M^*(C)$, which on-path data identify only by assuming optimality and which no public design observes at frontier scale
(Sections~\ref{sec:ident} and~\ref{sec:wedge}).
